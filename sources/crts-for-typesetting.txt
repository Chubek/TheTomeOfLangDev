CRT'sfor phototypesetting
J. C. A. C H A I M O W I C Z
One of the uses of CRT's in modern phototypesetting is for the generation of luminous images of characters
and other typographic symbols. These images are optically projected onto a web of photosensitized film or
paper a length of which, after exposure, development and drying constitutes the printer's master for platemaking. The typesetting is under computer control, backed up by magnetically stored typeface information
and software system support.
The paper reviews this technique and describes some of the means by which the demands on the CRT's,
their driving circuitry and the associated optics have been met in the more recent Linotype-Paul typesetters.

The strange thing about the particular type of displays
made by Linotype-Paul is that nobody looks at them.
Modern phototypesetting uses CRT's for the generation
of luminous images of characters and other typographics
symbols. These images are optically projected onto a web
of photosensitized film or paper a length of which, after
exposure, development and drying constitutes the
printer's master for platemaking. Nobody, however,
watches them while they are in the making.
Typesetting is computer controlled, backed up by
electronically stored typeface information. Page setting is
also computer controlled:- line spacing, 'justification'
(margin building) and 'hyphenation' (end of line word
break-ups) as well as copyfitting are programme controlled, with frequent use of subroutines. Figures 1 and 2
illustrate two examples of typesetters.

HARDWARE

STANDARDS

By hardware is meant the CRT, the circuits, the optics.
In order to cope with the task of generating characters as
small as 0.9ram and as tall as 50mm in latin, cyrillic and
many exotic alphabets, in hundreds of typefaces, to do this
with high regard to the aesthetic quality, with sufficient
detail and uniform brightness, and to locate the spot on
the screen with an accuracy of 10 "~ of its diameter the
CRT's must satisfy a set of stringent, demanding specifications and be driven by sophisticated circuitry. High
quality optics is required to preserve the hard earned
quality of the display on its journey from the CRT to the
paper. Without quality here, much valuable effort put
into the design, manufacture and final adjustments would
be squandered. Figures 3 and 4 represent samples of
typeset print-outs.

Anyone who has tried to transfer a waveform trace from
an oscilloscope onto a piece o f tracing paper by contact
will have noticed the line broadening effect. The phosphor
emits light into all directions o f a hemisphere and as the
paper is separated from it by the thickness of the faceplates
it is brushed not by the thin tip of a stylus o f radiation
but by the broad side of a cone. Visual parallax confirms
this. The thicker the glass the more severe the effects and
so a bad effect on the oscilloscope is even worse on a
phototypesetter CRT, where the faceplate can be over a
centimetre thick. This makes lenses necessary for any
kind of photographic recording o f a CRT trace, the lens
function being to image the phosphor display onto the
film. And y e t . . , on the Linotron 202 no lenses are used.
Figure 10 shows a short length o f an extra thin glass fibre.
Exploiting the phenomenon of total internal reflection
this 8/~m thick glass rod acts as a light guide. 'What comes
in goes out', provided the angle of incidence does not
exceed a certain maximum value - here lies the secret o f
the success of a CRT whose faceplate is made from a
multitude o f short glass rods fused together. The permissible cone o f incidence is far greater than the angle of
admittance o f an f/5.6 projection lens system would be.

The numerical aperture, NA of the material used in
the 202 is 0.66, corresponding to a flat angle of 41 °30'.
The light collecting angle of a lens system is vastly
inferior. Thus, here, more light reaches the photographic
emulsion, the CRT can be run at much lower cathode
currents, the spot can be sharper.
A multifibre pancake like the faceplate (Figs. I0 and 11)
is a highly anisotropic medium p e r se. In order, however
to enhance this anisotropy, a few 'dead' fibres are interspersed among the active, to stop whatever residual lateral
light propogation would take place, Fig. I 1.
Anisotropic light propogation almost entirely removes
parallax effects. At the same time halo effects are also
.minimized.
The individual fibres are so thin, that if all rods of a 202
screen were joined together end-to-end the resulting fibre
would be 620 miles long. As tile CRT spot itself is some
30 ~tm in diameter it falls on some 15 fibres at any one
time, and an averaging out of the fibre boundaries occurs.
removing the grainy structure of the image beyond
recognition.

Although tubes are available with ultra high resolution and
microspots often the tube mounting is almost as important
as the tube itself. This includes the various coils in their
boxes and gimbals, the magnetic shielding and, of course,
the main mount itself.
As a result, the CRT supplier needs skills well beyond
those of vacuum techniques, electron gun optics and
photometry, as what he sells is a complex, self contained
package. Examples of such packages are illustrated in
Fig. 12, while some unpackaged phototypesetting tubes
are shown in Fig. 13.
I may have given the impression that a typesetter is the
only piece of equipment needed for typesetting or at
least the only one using a CRT. This is certainly not so.
In a phototypesetting installation the typesetter is only
one of several member-units of hardware, as can be
judged by glancing at Fig. 14. Many of the remaining
units use CRT's. There, however, the CRT's are part
of interactive terminals and as such perform a VDU
function - a subject for a paper in itself.
