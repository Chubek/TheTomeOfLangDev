2013 Introduction
how did we get here?
I first used a Macintosh computer in 1992, in the basement computer
room of the University of Illinois at Champagne-Urbana. I was struck
then at the odd separation between the Macs in the middle of the room
and the PCs around the outside, circling the Macs, penning them in. There
were probably at that time more Macs than PCs, but the PCs were running
DOS, while the Mac OS was more friendly-looking and graphical.
All of my word processing up until that point had been on a dedicated
word processor, a glorified typewriter with a daisy wheel print mechanism
and a floppy disk drive to store the text files. I completed my first degree
using this machine, and wrote a couple of longer pieces on it, all lost
forever because of my inability to get the text files off the floppies once I
stopped using the machine. Before my word processor, I used a golfball
electric typewriter, and before that a manual. In the days of my manual
typewriter, if I wanted to produce anything fancy, I would go to W H
Smith and buy some Letraset transfer letters, then painstakingly rub them
onto paper. I was always vaguely unhappy with the choice of lettering
available on Letraset sheets.
As soon as I got my golfball typewriter, I became obsessed with getting
more golfballs, with different fonts. I bought a couple: one was just a
different typewriter face; the other was an italic – not a true italic, but an
oblique version of the standard Prestige or Courier. I would painstakingly
swap out the balls, switching between upright and oblique, in order to
produce what I considered more readable documents.
I lost this ability with the word processor. In theory, I could have bought a
couple of daisy wheels, but that would have defeated the object of being
able to keep revising a document and then hit “print” when it was
finished. So, on the day I stood in the U of I computer suite with a student
guide, I had an easy choice to make. I didn’t know how to use a computer,
but pointing a mouse and creating WYSIWYG documents to be printed
on a dot-matrix or laser printer seemed like the best option. MS-DOS
looked intimidating.

Of course, I immediately wasted loads of time formatting my document
before I even started work on it. I created test pages of all the different
fonts installed on the machine, and I even got to experiment with several
different word processors. It took me five minutes to learn to use the Mac,
and the next day it was I who acted as the guide for two or three other
exchange students who had also never used a computer before.
I immediately forgot this experience when I got back to England, and
carried on using my Panasonic word processor for a few more years.
When I started my Critical Theory MA degree at Nottingham University,
I decided it would be easier to have a proper computer. I asked the advice
of friends, and I was told to “get a 486”. I didn’t know what this was.
Luckily, my girlfriend had previous experience of Apple computers, and
she insisted we buy a Mac.
Of course, as soon as I was sitting back in front of one, I remembered that
I had done this before. I immediately set out to become an expert user,
and bought books (the Macintosh Bible), and purchased magazines:
Macworld, The Mac, MacUser, MacFormat. What I really loved about
these magazines were their cover disks, with demo software, utilities,
graphics, and free fonts.
Oh, the fonts. Collecting fonts became an obsession. I was struck
immediately that I was just as disappointed by the default font in my word
processing software as I had been by the Letraset letters available in W H
Smith. The reason, of course, was that it was the same font: Helvetica.
I hated Helvetica. And I hated Times New Roman. I didn’t know why. I
discussed it with my Dad, who started his career as a type compositor
when he was around 15 years old, and studied at the London School of
Printing. He didn’t share my hatred but revealed that in the print trade, a
sans serif like Helvetica was always referred to as a “grot.” Grot being
short for grotesque, meaning ugly.
Yes, I thought, Helvetica is ugly. And so is Times New Roman. But the
interesting thing to me was, why couldn’t anybody else see it? Helvetica
was even then the world’s most popular font. It was the default on every
Macintosh, was included with every laser printer, and had previously been
included on every Linotype machine.
Around this time, I was obliged to come up with a topic for my Critical
Theory dissertation, the 30,000 words or so that would help me qualify

for the next stage of academia: PhD research.
Still obsessing about fonts, I had discovered a section of the Nottingham
University library which was hardly ever visited. There was a whole row
of books about type. Blowing off the dust and looking inside them, it was
clear that nobody had taken any of these books out or at least 20 years.
Many of them were very old. I started to borrow them, reading avidly,
learning as much as I could, and trying to work out why it was that I hated
Helvetica and Times New Roman.
I was struck by two things. The first was that the entire conversation in
these books about printing, newspaper and magazines, book design, and
type seemed to rest on one key concept: legibility. In order to
communicate effectively, type needed to be readable. Good typography
didn’t get in the way of the writer’s message, it stayed in the background,
enabling thoughts and ideas to be transferred directly into the brain of the
reader. The job of the book designer especially was to act as an enabling
angel, making the process possible, but not seeking attention.
For a Critical Theory student, the idea that the words could just flow from
the page into someone’s head was like a flashing beacon. It was an
Emersonian concept: that words could refer to things in the world and that
meanings could be direct and unproblematic was clearly nonsense. And
yet here was the wisdom of hundreds of years of printing, that secretive
trade that just a decade before had been smashed by Eddie Shah, Rupert
Murdoch, and, yes, Steve Jobs.
The second thing that struck me was that nobody was talking about this.
People discussed writing and the word, and they referred to the
differences between speech and writing, the signifier and the signified,
denotation and connotation. All that. But nobody ever, ever mentioned
the visibility or invisibility of type.
This was big.
And then I came across a footnote. It was a throwaway comment from
French polymath and theorist Michel Serres, a “needless to say” which
struck me as being precisely wrong. Not just needful to say, but also a
whole field of research and understanding that required a lot of
unpacking.

I got my MA, achieved a Distinction (got away with it a bit, as you’ll
probably agree when you’ve finished reading this), and I went on to do
my PhD. What you’re about to read is more or less the text of my
dissertation, but don’t be concerned that it might be too obscure or
academic for the general reader. I don’t write like that. In fact, it’s fairly
obvious that I’m a lot more interested in type than I am in Critical Theory.

the status of type
In the translator’s Preface to Jacques Derrida’s Of Grammatology, Gayatri
Chakravorty ‘says’ it all:
...the name “writing” is given here to an entire structure of
investigation, not merely to “writing in the narrow sense”, graphic
notation on tangible material1.
Derrida’s book concerns itself for much the time with the repression of
‘writing in the narrow sense’; with the centrism that creates hierarchies of
speech over writing, signified over signifier, meaning over word; between
‘writing in the soul which is closer to the word’ and writing as ‘a dead
repetition of the word cut off from its source2.’ But even this
acknowledgement of the repression of the narrow, merely tangible, literal
idea of alphabetic writing slides unknowingly over the surface of another
meaning: the physical form of letters and their importance in
communication.
The French polymath Michel Serres also places writing – in the narrow
sense: I feel I must apply the qualification – in an opposition: this time to
printing, which he sets up as a solution to the problems of cacography – or
pathology in written communication. Printing ‘solves’ the problems of
writing-in-the-narrow-sense because it means that the reader no longer
has to decipher before understanding the words3.
Type is invisible. Start again: type in its narrow, merely tangible sense is
invisible: it is something we are trained to look through rather than at. We
look through it because it does not represent ‘an entire structure of
investigation’, it is only physical, nothing to do with out spiritual quest for
knowledge. We pass by onto the other side of the page, without letting our
eyes rest on what they have seen4. This is very convenient to the world
view (Rousseau's) that sees writing as a supplement to speaking. But if we
make type visible – by even talking about it – we create a huge problem
for this world view, which Derrida has already deconstructed. Do we need
to deconstruct further? Or deconstruct Derrida? Perhaps, because the

invisibility of type is also very convenient for those who wish to discuss
writing as an abstract concept — in writing.
When Derrida – and even Lacan – speak of ‘the letter’ they actually mean
alphabetic writing. They slide over the actual, concrete existence of ‘the
letter’ as if there was no doubt about what lies at the other end of the
metaphor. But a close look at the vast material concerning the printed
letter reveals that there are no grounds for believing this. In fact, there has
been almost nothing but controversy concerning letter designs – in the
twentieth and twenty-first centuries, more than ever.
In this work, I am concerned with the merely tangible aspects of the letter.
I want to halt that slide from printing, to writing, to ‘writing in the wider
sense’, and take a look at debates within the field of typography – bearing
in mind the work of Derrida, and especially Michel Serres, whose work
on noise and communications is especially pertinent. I believe that a
critical theory approach can illuminate those typographical debates – and
vice-versa.
I’ll begin with an examination of the extremes of typography: those print
environment in which type ‘suddenly’ becomes visible, something to be
looked at rather than through. In the middle section I want to show how
two strands of thought, which I characterise as enlightenment and
renaissance thinking, as well as technological changes, have affected the
design of letterforms. Of necessity, this section requires some delving into
history in order to show how constant this competition has been. Finally, I
try to find a third way, a different way of conceptualising letterforms,
which sees anexactitude as a virtue in the battle against noise and
confusion in written communication.

Part One
The Print Environment
the black box
WHEN APPLE INTRODUCED THE MACINTOSH with its GRAPHICAL USER
INTERFACE (GUI1) in 1984, the company made personal computing
attractive to a group of people who had previously found desktop
computers dauntingly counterintuitive and arcane. Indeed, home
computing was a realm of hobbyists and hackers, persons who combined
enthusiasm for home electronics with a knowledge of computer code: it
was, in short, an hermetic environment, a black box (with perhaps some
flashing lights) whose secrets were only available to the nerdish initiates
for whom time spent grappling with complex logic problems in binary
code was quality time.
The Macintosh was ‘the computer for the rest of us’, simpler to use and
with that stroke of genius from Xerox PARC the white virtual document
upon which black letters appeared as one hit the keys, just as with an
actual typewriter. Home computing slowly became more attractive to
‘creative’ people who prefer to wrestle, not with the syntax of computer
languages, but with words and pictures.
One of the most brilliant things about the Macintosh was that the words
one typed could appear on the screen in a variety of shapes and sizes; that
they could be emboldened or slanted, underlined, squeezed together or
tracked more closely across the page using commands within the font
and style menus.
The irony about this was that Apple had actually not opened the hermetic
black box of home computing. The secrets of coding and circuits
remained intact behind the GUI. Being able to switch the thing on and just
use it meant the people had less reason than ever to learn to communicate
with a computer. A black box that had been opened, however, was the one
marked Printing. With the introduction, the year after Macintosh, of the
first affordable small laser printer and a software program called
PageMaker, an hermetic environment that had been sealed for 500 years
was suddenly opened. Apple were offering forbidden fruit.

In breaking open that hermetic seal, the company that produced
PageMaker, Aldus, bridged the 500-year gap by evoking the name of one
of the most renowned printers of the Venetian Renaissance2. This
evocation of the distant past at the cutting edge of new technology is by
no means unusual in the history of printing. That it keeps happening is
one of the fascinating things about it. If printers have a god, then it is
surely Janus: looking forwards and backwards at the same time, with the
past joined to the present in a mysteriously unbreakable way. In the
history of the legibility debates I’m about to explore, the fiercest
confrontation usually occurs between those who view the past as a golden
age, who decry the decadence of the present, viewing historical time as a
descent from a former glory; and those who see the present as a
triumphant culmination of all that has gone before, with ‘us’ as ‘winners’
of a ‘race’ against our ancestors.

Representations of Janus3

Both camps oppose each other within the same framework of thought,
conceptualising time in a linear way: either as a descent from former
glory which requires renaissance or rebirth; or an ascent into
enlightenment, with scientific reason clearing away all the fallibility of
human irrationality. Sitting between the camps sits their unacknowledged
god Janus, and much more fluid conception of time, which is best
summarised by the French philosopher Michel Serres, who sees time not
as a line but as a chaotic flux which eddies and flows back and forth in
more random ways, making us at once ancient and modern:

Time is paradoxical; it folds and twists; it is as various as the dance of
flames in a brazier – here interrupted, there vertical, mobile and
unexpected. ❡The French language in its wisdom uses the same
word for weather and time, le temps. At a profound level they are the
same thing… ❡ Time doesn’t flow it percolates… In a filter, one flux
passes through, while another does not4.
We are used by now to the idea that weather systems are chaotic, with
sensitive dependence upon initial conditions, but it seems something of a
conceit (a party trick) to link this idea to time, simply because the French
have a shortage of words. But let’s go with it for now and see where it
leads.
The example Serres gives is of a ‘late-model car’ which contains
components from various stages of the development of transport: for
instance, the wheel, which is as ancient an invention as any. But it turns
out he could have easily given as his example a printed page in a book.
Such a page might well be produced using the very latest printing
technology on the high-quality modern paper now demanded by the
American National Standard for Information Sciences to ensure the
longevity of Printed Library Materials5, but though the type on the acid
free page might be digitally produced, loaded into the computer via a
wireless internet connection, it is as likely to be a design dating back to
1513 as one newly produced in 2013. Further, the colour of the print on
the page probably obeys ancient principles of layout and spacing, with the
appearance of the type itself owing much to the scriptural tradition it
superseded.
When the first Macintosh appeared, the typefaces included on its font
menu were named after cities:

The reason for this was that Apple wanted to cut costs by not licensing the
classic copyrighted faces owned by Linotype, Monotype and other
foundries.

Macintosh was viewed, after all, as a significant break with the past in all
other respects, the culmination of the postwar research into computing
that had taken place at such locations as Xerox PARC. There seemed no
need to reproduce classic typeface names in the new machine, so Apple’s
Susan Kare and others designed ‘knockoffs’ (above) which were
considered adequate.

That said, these designs weren’t exactly knock-offs – they weren’t close
enough to the originals to be mistaken for them by an expert. New York’s
x-height was much higher than that of Times New Roman. Geneva had
slightly less of the 19th Century about it than Helvetica, and tracked wider,
making it easier to read on a screen. The renaming was a break from the
past – as if the names of the originals had no real significance in the
information age. In choosing these particular names, however, Apple did
evoke certain feelings, associated with the city names. Geneva was, of
course, a sans serif face evoking Helvetica and the Swiss or
‘International’ typographical style. New York was a serif ‘book’ face
evoking Times and the Eastern seaboard literary scene. London was a
black letter gothic evoking the black-letter textura faces that were the first
moveable type used in Europe, but also the national handwriting of
England from the end of the 12th Century. San Francisco was an eclectic
mixture of different styles evoking a ransom note and the alternative
lifestyles of the West Coast (or even, perhaps, the instability of an
earthquake zone). If San Francisco was post-modern, then Chicago
evoked architectural modernity: it was a screen display font of modern
solidity, with more straight lines than curves in order to avoid the jaggies,
designed specifically to be the personality of the original Mac because it
appeared in every system and application menu.
If the team that produced the original Macintosh were in the
enlightenment camp, with no desire to revive the past, they still
understood that many of those ‘creative’ types who would be attracted to
using the machine were firmly in the renaissance camp. Steve Jobs
himself, only peripheral to the development of the Mac until its final

stages, had studied calligraphy and knew the importance of elegant type.
As Steven Levy writes in his book on Apple,
…people were going to learn more about fonts and typography using
the Macintosh than they had since Gutenberg… Fonts, which had
previously belonged to the equivalent of guilds – those with access to
Linotype machines and art directors and printing presses – now were
accessible with a single point and click… [The] fine china was now
available for everyday use6.
Fine china upon which to enjoy your forbidden fruit. It was a mixed bag,
in the end, of the brand-new (Chicago) with the ancient (London). There
was something for everyone. Just the idea that you might want to choose
from a variety of fonts was revolutionary. Why would you need different
fonts? Because if speech hides behind writing, writing hides behind
letterforms. And letterforms, far from being invisible, have personality.
They have identities, they evoke ideas, they create meanings, on their
own, independently of speech and writing. Even the language used to
discuss type is anthropomorphic. They have faces, names, even beards.
When some dyslexics see the letters and words dancing about on the
page, perhaps it’s because they perceive something that most of us do not:
that type is alive.
When Apple produced their first laser printer, they licensed Times and
Helvetica, not least because their ‘city’ fonts were unsuitable (at the time)
for printing at such a high resolution. With the introduction of the Apple
LaserWriter, the company was cooperating with Adobe by licensing the
PostScript page description language. Adobe also made available whole
software libraries of type in addition to the Times and Helvetica licensed
by Apple7. With PageMaker it was possible to print the virtual document
produced using the GUI in the typeface and style one had chosen, and to
see results that closely resembled the on-screen display. In fact, the
LaserWriter wasn’t the end of the story. Because of PostScript,
PageMaker documents could be produced on other printers, such as the
web offset machines used in magazine and newspaper production. The
advantage with the digital process was that it was possible to experiment
much more cheaply, to break with the ancient typographic and design
principles, to move decisively away from the physical limitations of hot
metal, to discover new principles (or rediscover the old ones), and to
achieve effects that were literally impossible before the digital age8.

One possibility allowed by digital type was negative leading. This was
physically impossible in the age of hot metal, when the beard of a type
would only allow the extenders of letters on adjacent lines to apparently
touch, negative leading allows words to crowd together and on top of each
other, appearing to blend and blur, creating new shapes, emphasising the
geometric forms of the letters in their relation to each other, and allowing
words to become more closely associated than they would normally be:
letters poking their faces out from the beards of others. Words are not
usually allowed to touch, but any touching leads inevitably to marriage
and a new identity: a brand name, a title, a catch-phrase, a logo. Negative
leading breaks down a hierarchy that has always previously existed,
where one line of type would sit rigidly above another on the page. The
only way to achieve such an effect with hot metal would have been to
deliberately overprint a page: something that would require great
precision and skill and lead to lots of wastage9.
Conventional wisdom has it that the decade following the introduction of
desktop publishing saw a flowering of creative typography like nothing
seen before. The doors to the closed shop of print had been flung open
and its secrets had been shared. Now, according to conventional wisdom,
anyone with a personal computer could be a designer, a typographer, or a
printer. With the advent of software programmes like Fontographer, or
more recently Font Lab Studio and TypeTool, anyone could design their
own types; could indeed simply scan their own handwriting to produce a
uniquely personal look to their printed output.

Given the fact that the task of creating a new typeface in the pre-digital
age could take years, and still today requires many hours of concentrated
work by (usually) more than one person, the omnipresence of what Vici
MacDonald calls ‘quick and dirty’ handwriting typefaces indicates that
although people are attracted to the idea of creating type, few understand
what it really entails10. It is not just a question of drawing an alphabet and
some punctuation symbols: the questions of spacing, alignment and
kerning need to be worked out. A professional typeface will contain

thousands of kerned pairs – and there’s no substitute for the human eye
when it comes to getting the proportions right.
Alongside such efforts are digital versions of type ancient and modern, a
variety of choice so extensive and widely available that – if types existed
in the physical world – the volume and weight of brass, lead, antimony
and other elements would be impossible to contemplate; the world would
surely be a giant lead ball floating in space. In the hot metal/typewriter
days, type had a sheer physical presence that has been lost in the digital
age, though we can be reminded of it when we use type in certain ways.

concrete poetry?
Much contemporary typography (so-called New Wave typography, but, as
we shall see, ‘new’ typography is a concept as old as the old typography
to the double gaze of Janus) resembles nothing so much as concrete
poetry. Typographers use their effects to achieve affect. To paraphrase,
they believe that printed information is experienced as well as read.
Typography is, according to Peter Hall, ‘the last secret weapon11’. But
such statements should give us pause. From the conventional wisdom of a
revolution in accessible, democratic typography where everybody can be
a designer and publisher, suddenly, it would seem, we are back to black
boxes, hermetic environments, secret weapons (secret from whom? who
keeps the secrets?). In thirty years which has seen the massive
dissemination of a once-exclusive craft, which has seen the virtual
destruction of the heavily demarcated print unions, which has seen big
printing works close, and hundreds of little print shops spring up to
replace them, surely this ‘secret weapon’ can no longer be?12
Such statements do, of course, arouse suspicion as well as interest. One
question that springs to mind, for instance, is to ask what is meant by
experienced in that statement about type being experienced as well as
read? Most people would contend that they do not notice type, so if it is
experienced at all then it is passively, invisibly, in the way that oxygen is
‘experienced’ when we breathe. Or perhaps, as Lacan suggests in his
essay, the symbol – the localised and concretised signifier – on the page
sets off a metonymic chain of association in the mind of the reader13. The
problem with such a construction, of course, is that I am taking rather

literally to think of letterforms when he writes, ‘the letter’. Neither Lacan
nor Derrida are thinking of type designs when they write of letters. Even
Derrida’s use of ‘the signifier of the signifier’ is a reference to alphabetic
writing in general, rather than letter designs in particular14.
What I am picking at here is an ignorance of what has been at stake in
letter design and even page layout over the history of print and
typography, Without detracting from Derrida’s analysis of Rousseau’s
Essai sur l’origine des langues, or his treatment of Levi Strauss with ‘The
Violence of the Letter’, I wish to pay closer attention to the letterform.
When a typographer speaks of ‘an alphabet’ they are not referring to
alphabetic writing in general, as it evolved from symbolic or pictorial
scripts, but to an actual, physical alphabet which they may have even
designed. Derrida, in considering writing as supplement, in considering
the ‘linguistic and metaphysical phonologism that raises speech above
writing,’ passes over the question of who designs an alphabet as if type
was transparent15.
In fact, it is the supposed transparency or invisibility of type in spite of its
obvious materiality that underlies many of the debates surrounding
typography over the history of printing. Developments in criticism over
the past few decades have exploded the myth of a transparent language,
but most people would probably accept without contention that the design
of the type in a particular book goes unnoticed, except by bibliophiles, or
in the most exceptional circumstances. Under the conventions of written
language, the reader ‘converts’ the sign of the word on the page into the
concept – or chain of associated concepts in a play of meanings – behind
the word as they read. Anything that would interfere with this process by
drawing attention to it actually breaks down the system, forcing us to look
at the type rather than through it.
I want to show that the trompe l’oeil that helps us ignore type is not
achieved so easily.
When typographers teach or write, it is usually to instruct in the choice of
the most appropriate type for a particular job, the correct proportions of a
page, the best method of layout, the most legible point sizes and the
optimum amount of leading. They usually have strong opinions (type
arouses passions), and are prepared to give reasons for them, but even the
most ardent advocates of experimentation and envelope-pushing would

advise the noviciate to stick to the rules until they know exactly how they
want to break them. In the basic typographical principles thus imparted,
the assumption is always that the ideas behind the words on the page are
more important than the physical form of the words on the page (no
matter how problematic the notion of elevating the signified over the
signifier/of the signifier), and that the type should not draw attention to
itself except in the specific circumstances of display.
That type arouses passions is important. It really does – but why does it?
Just type “I hate Helvetica” into Google and you will come across one of
the oldest debates on the internet. Type “ban Comic Sans” and you’ll find
another. You may be more or less dismayed to learn that when Pope
Benedict XVI announced his historical resignation in February 2013, the
Vatican published a commemorative online photo album with all the
captions set in Comic Sans. A pope resigning is a once-a-millennium
event, and here it is being marked with the worst font in the world.

Helvetica and Comic Sans are just the tip of the iceberg when it comes to
passions aroused by type. As the cartoon below from wondermark.com
shows, particular hatred is aroused by any typeface that is easily
recognised. In other words, the very types that are in everybody’s font

menu are the ones that arouse the greatest ire. This is appropriate, because
a line of type is a cliché. Yes, it’s a French word, meaning “une plaque de
métal qui permet le tirage d’une épreuve typographique.” In other words,
it’s a line of type. In English, a stereotype. An old printer’s trick when
dealing with lazy writers who kept using the same turns of phrase was to
hold on to the line of type so it could be re-used the next time the writer
used the same phrase. A typographical cliché would be to keep using a
recognisable font over and over again: it’s a mise-en-abîme, a recursive
hall of mirrors, cliché within cliché. The problem for Comic Sans is that
it’s instantly recognisable, and not just by experts. The same problem
afflicts Papyrus (as in the comic strip), which is over-used for exactly the
same reasons as Comic Sans – because it has the appearance of letters
drawn by a human.

Thus, the argument would go, we only notice type when the basic
typographical rules are broken: when the type is inappropriate for
purpose; or too small, or too large, for the size of the page; when there is
too much or not enough space between lines; when the length of the line
is too long or too short for comfortable reading; when the ink is
insufficiently black, or difficult to distinguish against a coloured

background; or the wrong type of paper has been used; or broken, wornout types.
It might help here to be clear on the ‘basics’ we are talking about. The
essential principles of ‘transparent’ typography are as follows.
There are various traditional sizes of paper known as quarto, octavo,
16mo, etc., but these have been replaced on the whole by the ISO A-series
of classification, where each Ax is twice the size of the next in the
sequence: A3 is twice the size of A4 etc.. Whatever the size of the paper,
the height to width ratio is the same (the square root of 2, or 1.4142). This
is useful for office applications, where you can trim sheets in two to get
two smaller sheets with the same proportions, or fold in two to file, or
reduce/enlarge when photocopying16. Whatever the size of the paper, the
print should appear on the page surrounded by margins of white space. In
the highest quality printing, the proportion of white space in relation
to total page size actually exceeds 50%. In the two volumes of Updike’s
Printing Types, for example, the page is 160mm wide and 235mm high.
The left and top margins are both 25mm; the right margin is 37½mm and
the bottom margin is 50mm. This arrangement gives marginal proportions
of 1:1:1½:2, though it is not unusual to find proportions of 1:1½:2:3. In
the case of Updike, the margins leave a text area of 97½mm x 160mm
(i.e. the body of the text is only as high as the width of the page), which is
156cm2 on a page area of 376cm2, or around 42% of the space available.
The ratio of height to width for the text on a page in Updike is 1:64:1,
which is not quite the golden ratio, but close.
As you can see from the diagram below, such proportions have the effect
of pushing the printed area upwards and to the left on the right hand page,
with a mirroring effect on its partner. This is so that the text is contained
within the overall shape of the book, and in a typical opening will not
appear to be ‘escaping’ over the edges into the background.

White space is also important in the design of the type used. The counters
of letters such as i, e, a, n, and m should not be so small as to become
clogged with ink, and should thus show some clear area of white: the type
design used to avoid clogging very much depends upon the quality of the
printing environment, with the cheaper, faster processes favouring more
‘open’ letters such as monotype’s Photina. Cheap paper is more
absorbent, like tissue paper, so the ink spreads, creating a softer outline to
the type. Looking at the thicker, lower-contrast strokes of 15th century
types, you’re also looking at ink spreading into paper, so you have only a
rough idea of what the actual type looked like in the metal. So when
versions of Jenson’s roman appear (see Part Two), they are only ever
interpretations, guesses based upon what the type might have looked like
on the higher-quality, smoother and less absorbent papers that came along
in the 18th century.

The line length of approximately 100mm in the Updike example given
above is ideal, as it gives a word count per line of approximately ten with
10 or 12 point type. Ten to twelve words per line is considered adequate,
while thirteen or fourteen is too many17. Depending on the width of the
column and the x-height of the letters, the ideal range of point sizes for
type is between 8 and 12. Studies of reading have shown that words are
absorbed in groups of approximately four, so that too narrow a column
will break up the flow of reading, with too many words broken with
hyphens; and too long a line will strain the muscles of the eye, forcing it
to move too far from the beginning to the end of each line, and creating
problems in finding the beginning of the next line. The amount of leading
is crucial, with 10 point ideally being set on 12 points of lead. Much
depends upon the x-height of the typeface in question, with leading
generally calculated as a proportion of x-height. Too much white space
between lines will again cause problems for the reader seeking the
beginning of the next line.
Such are the basic principles of ‘invisible’ typography, though I must
emphasise that the most important choice to make is the appropriate

typeface for the job in hand. The traditional basic advice for the
uninterested is to use Helvetica for headings and Times for text18, but
these choices can be elaborated upon almost infinitely, and the really
interesting questions arise not from which typeface to use, but the whys
and wherefores of preferences. Current typographical trends, for example,
tend to favour the sans serif Helvetica (or Helvetica-like) end of the type
spectrum – but why?
Apart from the selection of typeface, there are many other typographical
principles, of course, but already we can infer that even minor
contraventions of those principles already outlined are likely to affect the
reader in some way. In cheaper books, for instance, not only does the
quality of the paper suffer, but there are usually much smaller margins,
giving less surrounding white space. In such cases, there may be no room
for the reader’s thumb at the bottom of the page, forcing them to keep
shifting a book around to read hidden words. In addition, the type itself
may be too small to be read comfortably, or too tightly tracked. Modern
magazines often feature very small type as a means of cutting the overall
production costs, meaning that long-sighted older readers will struggle to
read anything other than the headlines. This is not as trivial as it might
seem. Small type in a book or magazine means that the volume will have
to be held closer to the face, causing exaggerated (and uncomfortable) eye
movements; further, the lack of surrounding white space will mean that
the reader’s peripheral vision of the background (the library, the living
room, the city park) will interfere with the text itself19.
And yet, some of these rules are broken quite deliberately by so-called
New wave typographers, and of course by poets wanting to draw attention
to the physicality of language. Concrete poets use a variety of techniques
to draw attention to the materiality of the signifier. This could involve
something as simple as rotating the paper in the typewriter, or even
blowing up the letters typed to reveal their rough edges. In such poetry,
the instrument used to make the words was as important as the words
themselves: technological poetry, machine poetry. Other poets were
concerned with creating calligrams: drawings of words which are what
the words mean. Concrete poetry can be a rejection of the abstract in
favour of the empirical, drawing attention to the actual lived experience of
language.

of which there is none in Furnival’s example as printed in the Williams

his tower. A book with a white margin has its message shielded from the
surrounding ‘noise’, set apart for purposes of clarity and legibility, in the
same way that an audience might hush for a spoken performance. Where
there is no white space, the colour of the page is explicitly being made to
blend with the surrounding colour in the world: bleeding causes the
miscegenation of colour. The communication on the page is fragile; it
needs to be protected, fenced in by white space. It is as fragile and
temporary as a tower, frozen in the moment of its fall.
The idea that beyond the ‘meaningless noise’ of the crowd at Babel lies
nothingness is also fascinating. The human organism produces many
chemical reactions and movements which of necessity produce
background noise, which we should be able to hear, but don’t. Some
people do in fact experience this noise as tinnitus, or ringing in the ears,
but most of us at some point experience what we think of as silence. In
fact, what silence actually represents is the absence of anything but the
essential background hum of the organism. This background hum can be
brought to our attention: for instance by John Cage, with his 4’33” of this
‘silence’. The changes that take place within this background noise are
what we experience as sensory perceptions and translate into language:
pleasure, pain, etc.. The ‘meaningless noise’ of Babel was always
language: its invention and use. The white space around the page of a

book, the margins of a document, are like a demilitarised zone between
the information on the page and the information in the world that would
otherwise drown it out.

Concrete poets, in testing typographical rules, question the authority of
the understanding we think we have when the rules have not been broken.
They show us an extreme, where we can ‘see’ type, and are supposed to
be looking at it rather than through it.

magazine design
The same ideas are played with, for much more commercial reasons, in
many contemporary magazines, especially American ones. In the first 10
years of the DTP revolution, due to lowering production costs and the
existence of specialist markets, there was an explosion in new magazine
titles. Due to their relatively small circulations, many were run on very
tight budgets22. Most newsagents now contain whole sections of titles
which simply not exist before 1985. For example, each home computer
and games console platform supports several different publications, with
usually one from each of the large publishing houses. There are also now
music and lifestyle magazines like Men’s Health, Q, FHM, Maxim, GQ,
Arena. Modelled on traditional women’s titles like Cosmopolitan, these
are aimed at a largely male audience, which ten or fifteen years before
were catered for only by soft pornography titles like Playboy and
Penthouse23. Away from the top shelf, the airbrushed models wear more
clothes and are surrounded by stylish typography. Letterforms are made to
bend and stretch, rotate an distort, revealing themselves in new and
interesting ways.
Concurrent with the expansion of the magazine market, there has been
increasing sophistication in news management and public relations. Even
the most minor and temporary celebrities tend to hire media spin doctors
to eke out their fifteen minutes of fame. In the host of titles on the shelf,
therefore, there is much repetition and imitation: not only the same stories

and faces in photographs, but similar design aesthetics. In their quest for
distinction and originality art directors have increasingly turned to type.
Peter Hall:
…diminishing magazine budgets for photography and illustration,
and the copycat tendencies of the media have forced art directors to
rely on proprietary type for a publication’s identity24.
Publications which have or had their ‘own’ typeface include Harper’s,
Time, Newsweek, and Life, as well as the smaller-circulation ‘lifestyle’
titles25. These latter tend to be far more experimental in their outlook,
seeking to stamp their alternative identities with alternative types.
In parallel with the Seattle based ‘grunge’ music scene of the early 1990s,
there was a blossoming of grunge or distressed typefaces, pioneered by
Ray Gun magazine, created by David Carson, whose design philosophy
was quite simple: you didn’t have to know the rules (as outlined above)
before breaking them26. I’m skeptical about the claim that you could
achieve what Carson did without knowing the rules – it’s obvious to me
that he knew them. You will have noted that he still acknowledged the
existence of rules. I’d further contend that Ray Gun’s readers also knew
the rules (absorbed from a lifetime spent consuming ‘invisible’
typography), which was how they recognised with pleasure that they were
being broken. We may be dealing with negative typography again, as with
the concrete poets, but we’re trapped inside the framework of thought that
created the rules in the first place. As Bob Dylan said, to live outside the
law, you must be honest. Ray Gun had its moment: 1992–2000.

These distressed typefaces are clearly to be viewed in opposition to
undistressed typefaces; in other words, ‘normal’ book faces. This is the
framework of thought within which the binary opposition operates. But
type also operates within another framework, one that is seen by some as
a prison. Whatever it is, in whatever style you use, type is always an
imitation of something else. Type is never its own thing. It is the strokes
of a pen or a brush, the mark left by a chisel, or whatever other “real”
implement that was used as inspiration for the type. To use an analogy
from recorded music, a “snare sound” on a commercial pop or rock record
sounds nothing like the sound of a real snare drum in the real world. It is

usually an improvement, but it still exists as a facsimile of the real thing,
in the way that type, which improves on handwriting, is still only an
imitation of handwriting.
Fonts like Trixie are designed to recreate the effect of old typewriters, and
the almost organic way the arms impacted the page, leaving in impression
so deep that the edges of the letters would bleed into the paper28. Clearly,
on one level, such fonts are a reaction against the clinical purity of the
output obtainable with a high resolution printer and a ‘classic’ typeface.
In print environments where such designs appear, some kind of individual
freedom is being expressed, the chains of the old rules being broken, but
also there was a sense of nostalgia for a time when we couldn’t control
the appearance of letters on the page. And yet we are, especially when
using a typewriter-style face in much larger sizes. Throughout most of the
history of the typewriter, each model only came with one standard-sized
type. The idea that you might reproduce, say, Courier, in a much larger
size (in signage for a bar/restaurant, for example), is actually quite
strange, if you give it some thought. Perhaps there’s also a nostalgia for
the days when the teleprinter would clatter out the football results on
Grandstand; for films like All The Presidents Men, in which a herojournalist could physically eject a president from office with the muscle of
his type; for days when a manuscript was different in appearance to the
finished article: something hammered out with blood, sweat and tears by
Kerouac on an old typewriter on the road. Further, the fashion for broken
or distressed types may evoke nostalgia for the days when Letraset
transfer lettering was all most people had – but Letraset was hard to align
and occasionally broke or split, no matter how careful you were.

But the design of Interference is also about rule breaking, about testing
the limits of legibility and questioning the validity of the old arguments. If
it is possible to create a book which breaks all the rules and can still be
read more or less comfortably, then the principles I laid out above can all
be called into question. In such circumstances, old style typefaces and
traditional ways of doing things survive for other reasons.
At these extremes, in these lawbreaking print environments of concrete
poetry and display advertisements it’s clear that type is calling attention to
itself in much the same way as one who is told to leave the room quietly
will then slam the door. When type draws attention to itself, it appears to
change, simply because the reader becomes aware of it. Something that is
normally transparent has become opaque. It is as if a line is crossed into a
new semiotic system, where the unconscious conversion of the printed
word on the page to the abstract idea in the mind is interrupted, and the
physical presence of the type on the page itself demands attention. But
where is the line? When is it crossed? How many rules can you break
without crossing it? Away from the theoretical, ‘unconscious’ or ‘natural’
reception of the words we cross to an empirical experience of these
words, on this page at this moment. Instead of meaning arising from the
differences between words and the letters making up the words, it is
created from the contrast between legibility and illegibility, decorative
and plain letters, between composition and display sizes. In this new
environment, we pay attention to the nature of printed matter: the
geometric shapes of the letters, the counters which should be white space
which perhaps are clogged with ink, the letters which would align with

each other and blend together to form words, but which instead hop about
the page with no discipline; the print which should sit crisply on a white
background, but which instead blends and blurs, the ink appearing to
spread into the fabric of the paper, or bleed into the margins. Faces that
bleed, which appear without their beards, which contort themselves and
entertain or paint themselves like clowns.
At these extremes of art and commerce, concrete poetry and magazine
design, it is clear that letterforms work in particular ways: to produce
affect, to assert an identity, corporate or otherwise. As mentioned above, it
is as if a line is crossed and what should be invisible becomes visible. But
such rule-breaking, because it still allows us to read the message with no
loss of legibility, is not simply prove (i.e. test) the rules: it throws them
into turmoil. The rules may be intended to preserve the invisibility of
type, but can it really ever be (as good as) invisible, or does it go on
working on the reader, even when they’re not aware of it? In other words,
is there really a line to cross?

authenticity
Another way of looking at typographical rules is to see them as preserving
not the aura of fine printing, but the craft of the printer. Printing, after all,
has had a turbulent history of labour and demarcation disputes. The first
printing shop did not open in Paris until 20 years after the introduction of
the new technology, simply because the scribes were such a powerful
lobby. In Paris, and in Lyon, there were labour disputes involving
journeymen, apprentices and local holidays, which went on for decades.
More recently, the introduction of new technology in the newspaper
industry caused mass redundancies, and long and bitter strikes.
The labour problem within printing has always been concerned with the
opposition between ‘skilled crafts’ and unskilled labour, and with the
placement of the dividing line between ‘craft’ and ‘mechanisation’ or
commercial production. From a Marxian perspective, mechanised labour
under capitalism is alienated, so any idea there is skill or commitment
involved has to be rejected as ideological and illusionary. The problem is
that a skilled craftsman or woman in any era, unless they are
independently wealthy – as was William Morris – needs to pursue that

craft for pecuniary gain. The culture of artisanal practices which Morris
preferred depends upon the existence of those hermetic environments
where skills take years to master, and are jealously guarded by those who
possess them. A master printer depended for their living on the possession
of skills which it was in their own interests to keep secret. Thus printing,
though always in the business of broadcasting information, always
maintained an aura around its practice which is the close relation of the
aura surrounding the work of art identified by Walter Benjamin in his
famous ‘Work of Art’ essay. It is an aura of authenticity supposedly
absent from the practices of production workers. An aura which
guarantees the authenticity of the crafted work, and which puts a premium
on the value of that work. Craft labour is worth more, and has a lasting
value, because skilled crafters are supposedly not alienated from the
product of their labour.
So it’s interesting that the fashion for ‘grunge’ types seen in the 1990s, or
the current trend for revivals of commercial sign-writing letters, and the
obsessive and irrational use of Comic Sans in the 21st Century also relates
to a notion of authenticity – something supposedly lost in the era of
digital type35.
The highly-paid magazine designer depends for their living on the idea
that they have an understanding of typography not possessed by the
person who cleans the office floor – or who buys the magazine. They
exploit this understanding in the same way as a concrete poet – neither
would want to admit that there weren’t rules which only an expert could
break and still produce something worthwhile36.
Interestingly, it is this same demarcation question which lay at the root of
the printing disputes of the early-mid 1980s when first the Today
newspaper (run by Eddie Shah) and then News International newspapers
like The Times (led by Rupert Murdoch) were involved in long and
sometimes violent trade union disputes over the introduction of new
technology. It was not so much the technology which was at issue as the
use of certain types of labour. The printing unions wanted to preserve
demarcations which went back hundreds of years; in other words, they
wished to maintain the aura surrounding their work, an aura shattered not
by the new technology itself but by the kinds of people who were being
brought in to run the software: members of the electrician’s union.

Ideas of authenticity within artisanal practices point to an area of
knowledge which is exclusive to the artisan: the secret weapon of affect
which exists in typography. If type was truly transparent, this area of
knowledge could not exist.
Although Interference sets about breaking the rules, it does so in a
particular context: the ‘art’ book. Art and photography is often
typographically represented in such a way as to distinguish it from, say,
run of the mill fiction. These are paid professionals, type stuntmen, being
allowed to break the rules. Don’t try this at home: I’m an artist. ‘Art’
exists at the extremes and is imitated in glossy magazines: after all, these
are the two print environments where words and pictures tend to co-exist.
The materiality of the pictures is evoked along with the materiality of the
words. Type is allowed to be visible when we are supposed to be looking.
It is, however, only ever a convention that stops us looking at other times,
a convention signalled to us by the disposition of the information on the
page: the size and design of the type, the amount of white space, the
quality of the paper.
I find it fascinating that we can hold something in front of our eyes that
we are not supposed to notice. In the sections that follow, I hope to show
– firstly – that the idea that type is ‘invisible’ is a false one. It may seem
as if I have been setting up a straw man in this respect, but the many
expert books on typography, dating back a hundred or more years, all
made the case that book and newspaper types in particular are meant to be
invisible. I hope to show that readers notice much more than is generally
believed, and that it is not just in ‘display’ and ‘poetic’ environments that
type calls attention to itself37.
Secondly, I hope to explore the problematic idea that type always reflects
the era in which it is produced. Thirdly, I want to examine the thinking
that lies behind such ideas, thinking that can often be characterised, as I
have already indicated, as a dispute between enlightenment and
renaissance, but which is really part of the same ‘logocentrism’ described
by Derrida as ‘an attempt to trace the sense of being to the logos, to
discourse or reason… and which considers writing or technique to be
secondary to logos.’ In the case of typography, the technique is the
technology of type itself. Finally, I would like to suggest that there is a
third way: a route through the impasse, which perhaps only Janus can see

clearly. This would be a way of looking at type that removes it from linear
time and says more about the nature of letters, noise, communication, and
perhaps even reason itself.

WHEN THE ENGRAVER AND GOLDSMITH JOHANN GENSFLEISCH aka
Gutenberg invented movable type in 1454, the letterforms he used were
the black letter ‘Gothics’ which were then current in the Northern Alps.
When William Caxton stated the first English printing press (1477), he
too used Gothic types. Both of these Northern European print pioneers
were doing what printers have always done: imitating something else – in
this case, the contemporary book hand which was pejoratively called
‘Gothic’ by the Renaissance Humanists, who had similarly labelled the
Ogival style of architecture; both styles were considered ‘barbaric1’.

It’s all gothic2

In the Southern part of Europe at around the same time, the Humanists
were favouring a revived ninth century handwriting style known as the
Carolingian (or Caroline) minuscule. So, even though black letter gothics
were ‘current’ in Italy3, early Italian printers also experimented with types
based on this extremely legible revival, and, apart from Germany (where
the Gothics or ‘Fracturs’ remained in common use until the 1930s), the
spread and popularity of reading stemmed from the use of the rounder,
more user-friendly ‘roman’ letters4.

Carolingian minuscule - dating from 800–1200 ad

What is today called ‘roman type’ evolved in the city states of Italy,
between 1465 and 1470, out of an odd combination of Roman
inscriptional majuscules (capital letters), dating from the period of
Trajan’s column (early 2nd century AD) and the Renaissance revival of the
Carolingian minuscules. The smaller letters were not based on
inscriptions but on the more rounded hand which came into use after the
revival of learning initiated by Alcuin of York, abbot of St. Martins at
Tours in the reign of Charlemagne, 796–804 AD5.
This clear and beautiful alphabet, in which every letter is distinctly
formed, spread rapidly over Europe, and is the parent of modern
script and print6…
But it is not enough to merely mention, ‘inscriptional capitals’ and
‘Carolingian minuscules’, for it seems to me that the combination of these
two letterforms in the roman printing alphabet is almost miraculous.
Updike goes into exhaustive detail on the development of the Latin
alphabet, breaking it into its component parts in his Socratic way (Latin
writing has five periods; the first period has five subdivisions; the first
subdivision has three further subdivisions; etc.)7.

The inscriptional capitals from Trajan’s column

What one learns in studying this history is that the square, upright,
inscriptional capital letter was only one choice out of many; that there
were other, rounder, more cursive capital forms which were used both
formally and informally. Capital letters – on their own – were used for
special purposes: for titles, for formal documents; sometimes they were
used for decorated initial letters, also, but it was just as usual to see the
use of a more ‘rustic’ letter for this purpose.
As to minuscules, they have had a serpentine career. Aside from the
development of Roman capitals, the ancient cursive handwriting evolved
through the history of the Rome, and in its later form was fairly standard
across Europe until the fall of the Empire. There then followed a period of
national differentiation, when the handwritings of Europe developed their
own idiosyncrasies. In 789 when Charlemagne issued his decree that
Church books should be revised (because many copies of texts were
inaccurate or corrupted), the script used at Tours was not the recent
Merovingian letter but a revival of later Roman half-uncial letters. It is
important to note that the Caroline minuscule was itself a revival – albeit
not a slavish one – of that earlier hand, chosen for its legibility in the
ancient books the scribes had before their eyes.

Merovingian handwriting (6th century)

Carolingian handwriting (9th century) - note that the “capital” letters used here are uncials

After this handwriting spread across Europe following the revival of
learning, it again underwent national differentiation. Such differentiation
always involves innovation, decoration, and the addition of flourishes.
The later ‘Gothics’ were in fact bastardisations of the earlier Caroline
hand, which underwent its second revival at the time of the Italian
Renaissance8. A third century writing style, revived in the ninth century,
revived again in the 15th century, borrowed for typographical purposes,
and then revived in the early 20th century, and yet again in the 1990s. A
revival of a copy of a revival, a recursive trip into the abyss.
This is a story about the centre and its ability to hold on, to exercise
control. It’s also a story about the power paradox of mass media.
Charlemagne’s decree was designed to allow for the easy communication
of information, instructions, orders. By stipulating the most legible hand,
the centre was able to ensure that everybody who was able to read would
indeed be able to read. The mass media of the time was of course the
Catholic mass itself. But the paradox created by legible handwriting is
that knowledge is power, and the more widely distributed the knowledge,
the more dissipated the power. Literacy encourages people to read, and
people who read also think. Those who wish to conserve their power have
a need to keep knowledge to themselves. Legible handwriting is
effectively more democratic – the more quirky and individual it becomes,
the harder it is to read. But your signature is also your security: you want
it to be illegible, so it is hard to copy. So it is with handwriting: when the
local official wants to grab a little bit of power, the instructions suddenly

become a little harder to read, a little more ambiguous. The degradation in
the Carolingian hand mirrored the slackening of central control. As
handwriting becomes more and more ‘Gothic’, fewer and fewer people
are able to read and write: knowledge becomes the preserve of those in
the know.

The paradox of roman types then, is that the shapes of modern capitals are
derived, not from the attractive curved uncials developed by scribes
during the 4th and 5th centuries (which were usually paired with the
Carolingian minuscule in writing), but from square or geometrical forms
incised on stone with a chisel. This choice becomes more understandable,
however, when we remember that the craft of cutting punches for letter
matrices is derived from the fine metalwork of the goldsmith, requiring
skill with a graver and cutting tool rather than pen and ink. On the other
hand, the smaller characters of the ‘lower case’ still preserve much of the
quality of handwritten letters, notably the oblique thickening produced by
the split and angled nib of a reed or quill. The reason for the success of
the Caroline minuscule is its legibility. When handwriting styles
differentiate along national lines, they assume the qualities which make
them hard for outsiders to read. Updike cites Hall:
“Even the painstaking scholars of the Renaissance were completely
at a loss when they were confronted by the Irish hand, or the
Lombardic9…”

It’s important not to underestimate the importance of legibility in the
development of printing. It may seem obvious today, but any person
confronted with one of the early examples of printing using Gothic type
knows the difficulty in making out the words on a sheet which has the
appearance of sharp vertical lines ranging down the page rather than
across. Before the invention of printing, if a person wanted to own a book,
they simply copied it – in their own hand – and it was not necessary for
anybody else to be able to read it. For much of history, the problem of
academics and scholars in advance of concerns with structures of meaning
was in encountering the indecipherable – which is why texts had become
inaccurate and corrupted at the time of Charlemagne.

According to Michel Serres,
Writing is one of the simplest methods [of communication] and, at
the same time, one of the richest, since I can store, transmit, and
multiply information with it. But before entering into these problems,
as well as those of style, the disposition of the narrative, of
argumentation, and so on, there is first the physical appearance of
the writing, its graphic form… For the moment, let us agree that
written communication is only possible between two persons used to
the same graphic forms, trained to code and decode meaning by
using the same key10.
Serres argues that ‘written communication is the battle of the scribe and
the reader, joined together by interest.11’Before the spread of literacy,
reading and writing were the preserve of an elite. Literacy was not just a
question of being able to read, but decoding the form of the words as
written in the current national style. If the storage of information was the
only concern, then a condensed shorthand would be adequate. If a text is

meant to be read, however, then legibility matters. Serres argues that
writing is drawings of words:
The scribe must execute his drawing as well as possible… The form
of the letters (standardised), probably formed clusters of letters and
of words (regulated by the rules of morphology and syntax), and so
on. [The graph] also comprises inessential, accidental graphic signs,
those without meaning whose presence depends on the ability, the
clumsiness, the education, the passion, or the illness of he who
writes: waverings in the graphic forms, failures in the drawing,
spelling errors12…

the search for the ‘universal’ type
I will turn again to Michel Serres, but for now it is enough to note that his
words above, on the subject he calls ‘cacography’ (the study of the
pathology of written communication), raise important issues. It has been
exactly this subject of the ‘essential’, ‘standard’ form of letters and words
and they way they should appear on the page which has vexed
typographers since Gutenberg. Who decides these important questions?
Most of us would agree that Gutenberg got it wrong: he chose to imitate
the national handwriting style in his Bible of c.1454 because it was the
only model available to him. Gutenberg’s imitation of hand-scribed books
went as far as decoration and illumination, added painstakingly by hand
after the main text was printed. So it was that from the moment of its
birth, printing was always trying to be something else.

When the fifteenth century types were revived, there was a need to
include with them an italic. Modern conventions of type dictated that each
roman should have a companion italic. This was difficult, however,
because italic type had not yet been ‘invented’ when Jenson struck his
roman; and the custom of pairing an italic with a roman was not adopted
until later in the sixteenth century.
Italic types, so-called because of their Italian Renaissance origin and to
distinguish them from the more ancient Roman letters, were developed
originally by Aldus as separate types for use in whole books, rather than
as emphatic complements to roman faces as they are used today. They are
more compact than roman letters, and were originally intended for the
proto-paperbacks (smaller sized, cheaper editions for general readers) of
the Aldine Press17. Italics were based on a cursive hand which originated

in the Papal chancery, and were used for the supplementary ‘briefs’ that
accompanied Papal Bulls (which were written in a form of Gothic18).
Italics were yet another example of printers imitating something else, in
this case, the current Italian informal cursive hand19. When you consider
that some of the earliest (and most beautiful) italic types originated in a
handwriting style used in the Vatican, it seems even more tragic that they
should choose Comic Sans to commemorate the resignation of Pope
Benedict.
Italic letters can be viewed, in their original form, as an independent
alphabet in competition with the Caroline Roman and Gothic lettering
systems. In fact, all three kinds of writing – together with capitals – were
used for special purposes, and to the contemporary eye the forms of the
letters would have acted as a signal to the reader, before they began to
read, indicating that type of material they were dealing with – as well as
perhaps any ‘right’ they may have had to read it. For why would a Papal
bull be written in perhaps difficult-to-read Gothic, while the
supplementary information was written in a more legible cursive style?
Perhaps because the supplementary information was meant for the eyes of
lesser officials. The more upright and formal a letter (roman, gothic), the
more official it is. The more it slants or leans, the more casual. Readers in
the 15th century would have recognised the distinction; I’d argue that most
people still would. Why else would all those school and examination
achievement certificates be headed with gothic lettering?
As I mentioned before, a modern equivalent this practice of excluding
people with handwriting was the illegible script used by NHS doctors
when writing prescriptions: meant for the expert eye of the pharmacist.
The changes in the health system over recent years have seen most
doctors printing their prescriptions by machine. This practice goes along
with the greater openness demanded as a ‘right’ by patients. But anyone
who visits a hospital and tries to read a doctor’s notes will still encounter
this cacography.
When Aldus used italic in his populist pocket editions, he was implicitly
stating that it was acceptable for a wide readership to carry this kind of
book around in a pocket or saddlebag – in contrast to the more
sacred/secret kinds of books, which were read at tables and lecterns. Italic
types, with their rakish, leaning style, were implicitly democratic.

Today, there is a clear hierarchy in alphabets, with italics rarely used as
anything other than a supplement to a roman type, and the use of ALL
CAPITALS restricted for VERY IMPORTANT NOTICES or for
SHOUTING on Twitter.
According to Updike, Romans used scriptura cursiva for ‘letters,
accounts, receipts, and scribblings20.’ When the scribes at Tours prepared
their new texts, they did in fact use at least three styles of writing,
including an elegant ‘display’ letter for passages ‘which it was desired to
bring into prominence21.’ Throughout the history of writing, then,
different letterforms were used for different purposes, and this practice
has been carried over into printing. Most people are familiar with display
types in larger sizes, but are we perhaps growing less aware of the special
use of other letterforms? I will discuss this further in Part Three, but it
seems clear that the conventions of formal and informal writing have
broken down somewhat in the era of Comic Sans. The relationship of
convention/rule to cliché/stereotype is also difficult to parse. For some,
roman letters with serifs are as annoying a cliché as a staff notice printed
using Comic Sans or a film title sequence that uses Papyrus. For the
purposes of display, the search for novelty in type is endless, because
nothing has less staying power than a novelty. Types like Bembo or
Garamond which have survived for 500 years, have something more than
novelty.
If all this talk about separate and competing alphabets seems
unconvincing, you should noted that when italic type was first used, it
was paired with the same upright roman capital letters that had previously
been paired with Roman minuscules. There was no such thing as an italic
capital letter type – and the equivalent cursive or rustic capitals may have
been considered either too difficult to make, or more probably, too
difficult to read. A passage of early italic printing then, would have
looked something like this, complete with awkward letter-spacing created
by non-matching types:

Something which should always be borne in mind when considering
printing types is the economic context. Aldus was producing the first
‘cheap’ books, and his choice of italic type – apart from its informality –
was also designed to save space. Italic letters are narrower than roman,
and so more words can be fitted per line, allowing smaller pages, and
lighter books. Many of the distortions that type underwent from 1500 to
1900 can in some way be attributed to this need to save money and
material. Although printing types were always imitating something else
(first of all, the manuscripts of scribes, later on earlier book types and
‘fine printing’), the overriding reason for that imitation was economic: a
few printers producing many volumes in a fraction of the time it would
take a whole scriptorium of scribes; and later, an almost completely
mechanised printing press producing books to match the quality of a hand
press. As we will discuss shortly, when William Morris turned to printing,
he explicitly printed imitations of hand-decorated ‘poetry books’.
Before considering the distortions referred to above, I wish to consider the
way in which italic types were absorbed by the roman alphabet, so that
they became, not a competing and different form type, but a contrasting
and complementary alphabet, part of the printer’s array of ‘secret
weapons’. The person generally attributed with the first combined Roman
and italic type is François Guyot of Antwerp22. The main point is that,
while italic type appeared 30 years after the first roman, it was another 30

years before the first slanted italic capitals appeared in Lyon and perhaps
another 15 years before italic was paired with a roman.
In becoming ‘complementary to the roman’, italics moved away from the
form in which they were based upon the chancery cursive style, and were
based – most notably in Robert Granjon’s Civilité – upon a later cursive
style of handwriting23.

The flip side of economic necessity is the legibility factor. Economy-withlegibility is a complex equation. Space saved in one direction, for
example by using a condensed typeface, usually has to be lost in another
direction, for example by adding more leading. Although Aldus had used
them to save space and money, italics didn’t catch on as book types,
simply because they are too hard to read for long passages of text24. So
roman endured as the main book type, but designers continued to tinker
with it in the hope of incorporating into it the condensed virtue of italic
and creating that chimera: the type that is both legible and economical.
The degradation in type design and use in the nineteenth century is
usually attributed to economic factors: the need to save money and
material, and the exigencies of industrialisation, which saw the printing
process increasingly mechanised and accelerated. It is one of the
paradoxes of printing, however that a new technology will often see an
improvement in quality, only for there to follow a degradation in that
same quality when the new process is – perhaps – incorrectly or hurriedly

applied – or even ignored altogether. Many of the printing problems of the
nineteenth century can be put down to the fact that old type designs were
too fragile for the tough treatment meted out by the high-speed printing
press. As Eric Gill noted in his Essay on Typography:
A print is properly a dent made by pressing; the history of letterpress
printing is the history of the abolition of that dent25.
Print comes from imprint. The original printing press was based on the
kind of press that was used in wine or oil-making. Over the centuries, the
impression of printing grew more and more shallow, until it became as
light as a kiss on the paper (kiss impression). The lighter the touch, the
quicker the process. The abolition of the dent was a side effect of the need
to speed up the process. Type designs are often changed to meet the
unforgiving needs of new technology. On the other hand, some type
founders produce types which use new technologies to their fullest extent
– and consequently only look their best in the highest-quality print
environments. For example, the Birmingham printer John Baskerville is
not only credited with a fine roman type which is still in widespread use,
but also with the mixing of the first truly black ink, and the introduction
of the process of ‘calendering’ paper26. He was probably the first printer
since Gutenberg to consider the whole process in such detail.
Baskerville was unusual for his understanding of the links between
readability, the pressure applied to the type and the surface of the
paper. By heating the paper between hot copper plates (calendering)
he was ale to create a smoother surface so that less pressure was
needed on the type. This is turn meant that the serifs could be more
delicate, a trend which culminated in the hairline serifs of Bodoni
and Didot at the start of the 19th century27.
Baskerville’s type exhibited a far greater contrast between thick and thin
strokes than had earlier romans. His letters also had a more ‘engraved’
quality, with a vertical stress rather than the oblique stress of the more
calligraphic types. John Baskerville’s innovations went largely ignored in
his own country, but were later picked up, first in France by the Didots,
and later in Italy by Bodoni. It’s at this stage that type begins to be truer to
its own nature (as an engraved artefact) – the house being rebuilt from its
foundations.

Baskerville is now classified as a ‘Transitional’ type; which is to say, a
halfway point between Old Style humanist romans like Bembo and
Garamond and true moderns like Didot and Bodoni.

What is modernism in type design? It is the
recognition, three hundred years after the invention
of movable type in Europe that type is not
handwriting. After three hundred years of variations
on the same theme (an imitation of renaissance
humanist scripts), type founders started to design
letters that owed their form, not to the split nib of a
quill, but to the engraving tools of the punch cutter.
In attempting this project, typographers of the 18th century were – as
Michel Serres might put it – attempting to rebuild the house from clean
foundations, knocking it down to the ground in order to rid it of rats and
other parasites. In printing terms, the foundations were old handwritten
letters like Caroline minuscule or Roman inscriptional lettering, and the
rats in the foundations were all the quirks of those methods: the serifs, in
particular, but also the angle of strokes and stroke contrast. Modern type
acknowledges that it is engraved and not drawn, but the model is still
there, for what else are these letters but a variation of the Caroline hand?
What else is the italic other than a variation of Granjon’s Civilité?

for a stone cutter to finish off a letter in an inscription. As we shall see,
the ‘enlightenment’ mind has in the intervening years often recoiled from
what are seen as these unnecessary ‘excrescences’ on the pure functional
form of a letter, these rats in the foundations. Capital A, so the argument

So it’s not as simple as three strokes of a pen. It’s also the case that type

letters, often attached to the upright with a bracketing curve). Bodoni, the
Italian modernist type designer, took the idea of the serif to its furthest
extreme. Bodoni’s book of type samples featured a variety of different
types, including non-Latin alphabets, and some with more traditional
bracketing – but some of his punches were so delicately cut that the serifs
survived only as the finest lines. Some fonts show no bracketing or
thickening of the serif as it joins the upright main stroke of the letters. An
enlargement can do justice to the difference (see below).

Plantin type, on the other hand, is squat, solid, robust-looking, exhibiting

a reduced contrast in stroke width (the difference between the thickest and
thinnest parts of the letterforms is not so pronounced).
Apart from the reduction of the serifs to delicate lines, the emphasis in the
strokes of the Bodoni letter are more upright, and the contrast is much
more emphatic. The traditional humanist roman letter as formed by a
scribe is written using a pen with a nib angled at 45°. Thus letters with
round elements such as d or o have a stress slightly off vertical, with the
thinnest part of the curve coming not perpendicular to the BASELINE, but at
a slight angle from it. Beginning with Baskerville, the angle of the pen
began to change. In Bodoni’s types, the stress in the letter o is entirely
vertical, so that the letter is both more upright and narrower than the
Plantin o28. In drawing his letters this way, Bodoni created a high contrast
between the thin and thick strokes, meaning that the printing conditions
became crucial to the proper reproduction of the letters. A poor printing
environment could lead to the delicate hair serifs disappearing entirely, or
being rendered as a blur rather than a straight line. Like Baskerville before
him, Bodoni was noted for the quality of his inks and papers. His book of

Away from the display environment, the character of the composition
types was meddled with, and so-called ‘legibility’ types came into
existence. These were a series of largely anonymous designs aimed at
fitting as many words to a column inch as possible. These types
dominated newspaper printing in the nineteenth century in Britain and the
USA. For this reason, and because printers had very few other types
suitable for the purpose, the quality of book printing also suffered a
degradation at that time.
Type is itself a discourse. The choice of typeface, it’s size, colour,
spacing, and layout, all speak both to the reader and of the attitudes and
values of the producers of printed material. The changes in type over
centuries reflect changing values and priorities as well as ideas about
methods of communication.
The nineteenth century prioritised industrial production and efficiency –
and above all profit-making – over the human element. The resulting poor
craftsmanship created ugly newspapers, garish advertisements, and badly
printed books. Underlying this creative crisis was the profit motive and
almost complete contempt for the audience.
Into this vacuum of poor craftsmanship stepped two solutions to the crisis.
The first was the ‘private press’ aspect of the Arts and Crafts movement
pioneered by William Morris. Dissatisfied with the published appearance
of some of his early books, Morris set about learning all he could about
punch cutting and type founding, using at his Kelmscott Press the Golden
Type, which was based on Jenson’s 1470 roman. Surrounded by
decorative woodcut prints, a page of a Kelmscott Press book broadcasts
its hand-set, home-made qualities, something Morris deliberately set out
to do, according to Jerome McGann:

Other members of the private press movement rejected the overt
decoration of the Kelmscott Press and produced finer versions of Jenson
(such as the aforementioned Doves type). The private press movement as
a whole had a curiously ambivalent attitude to the question of reviving
types, however. Certainly this movement could be seen as part of the
renaissance camp, and yet many of its type designers tended to argue
against the ‘slavish’ copying of designs. Morris’s own type tended to look
crude in comparison to types cut by, say, Eric Gill.
Figures such as Gill, Stanley Morison (in the UK), Morris Fuller Benton
and Frederick Goudy (in the United States), in spite of their private press
connections, were happy to produce both new designs and revivals for
such companies as Monotype and Linotype: whose machines represented
the second solution to the crisis afflicting printing in the nineteenth
century. Both were devices to set type by machine rather than by hand.
Together with the punch-cutting machine invented in 1885 by Morris
Fuller’s father Linn Boyd Benton, they represented a further automation

of the printing process. As such, they might have been viewed as part of
the problem of industrialisation rather than the solution.
Ironically, however, the new typesetting machinery was largely
responsible for the revival in fine type design in the early part of the 20th
century. As already mentioned, the era can be viewed as yet another
Humanist revival of the Caroline minuscule and its overriding reason for
being: legibility. In order to persuade printers that they wanted to invest in
the new machinery, both Monotype and Linotype embarked on a
programme of type revivals. The British Monotype Corporation under
Stanley Morison, in particular, was very ambitious in its aims. In
beginning the revival, and in promoting their machines, the companies
were explicitly competing with the ‘fine books’ of the private presses.
Inevitably, the economic power of the large corporations had a crushing
effect on small presses. On the other hand, the quality of the new types
was undeniable.
Interest in type design grew so much that in 1929, a special edition of the
London Times was given over to the subject of printing. In an article in
that number, Morison took the newspaper to task for its poor and out-ofdate typography. He was commissioned to produce a new type and on
October 3, 1932, the Times appeared in its new clothes for the first time:
Times New Roman. There are several ironies involved in the introduction
of this ‘new’ ‘up-to-date’ typeface. The first is that, although this was a
new type, it was based on Monotype’s 1913 revival of Plantin, which was
first cut by Claude Garamond in about 1550. Times New Roman was
given a higher contrast in stroke weight and sharper serifs (meaning it
showed a better colour on the page), but these modifications only
represented what was possible in the 20th century printing environment. It
has to be remembered that Christopher Plantin, in 16th century Antwerp,
was working in the pre-Baskerville era, when pages were subject to ink
squeeze and Gill’s dent.
The second irony is that Times New Roman was actually unsuitable for
the printing equipment used by The Times. While the Monotype machine
was used in the book trade, newspapers generally used the Linotype
machine, which set whole lines of type in slugs rather than individual
letters. Morison, used to working for Monotype, oversaw a design with

some characters too delicate for Linotype setting and high-speed
newspaper printing.

What Ashley means by ‘the instrument’ is the tool used to create the
appearance of the letterforms on the page: from the triangular-tipped
stylus pressed into clay by the Mesopotamians to OpenType fonts on the
Apple Macintosh and laser printers with coated paper.
For instance, Egyptian hieroglyphics, argues Ashley, are actually rounder
and drawn faster than cuneiform figures, because they were formed using
a reed brush on papyrus. Following the Egyptians, the Phœnicians (1300
to 100 bc) developed a system of writing where just 22 symbols – each
representing a sound – could be used to store and transmit messages. This
form of writing saves space as well as time, and the modern roman, greek
and cyrillic alphabets all derive from this ‘cost effective’ system29. When
the prototypical roman capital letters were carved into Trajan’s column in
ad 114, they had first been drawn with chalk (to ensure spacing and fit)
and then painted with a chisel-shaped brush, which naturally resulted in
letters ‘which had both thick and thin strokes30’. When the stone-cutter
finished off these letters, it was a natural step to finish off these thick and

thin strokes with a chiselled end mark: the very end mark which has
persisted as the serif on many letters to this day.
Ashley’s analysis points up the changes in letter styles which resulted
from new technology and gives credence to the arguments of the those
who contend that each era produces types to reflect the spirit of its age.
This recalls the copy in the Fontworks advertisement mentioned in Part
One:
… typography always reflects contemporary culture. Gutenberg’s use
of type reflected the work of the scribes. Bodoni’s layouts and types
fitted the grace, style and architecture of 18th-century Italy.
Really there are two things happening here. On the one hand is Ashley’s
fairly concrete examination of the instrument used to form letters, the
paper which they appeared on, the speed at which they were produced.
Thus the scribes Gutenberg imitated were using a goose quill to form the
narrow, upright Gothic letters of the Northern Alps. The narrowness of
these letters was a function of the need to conserve space when materials
were expensive. The shapes of these letters were ideal for Gutenberg,
enabling him to print his 42-line Bible in two columns. Rounder or wider
letters would have been prohibitively expensive.
On the other hand is the more nebulous idea, where Gutenberg’s entirely
practical and circumstantial imitation of scribes becomes – by the time of
Bodoni – a reflection of classical architecture, or – with the 90s trend for
distressed types – a reflection of urban decay and ‘grunge’ culture. As I
have already pointed out, however, both Bodoni and Baskerville were
exploiting the full capabilities of new technology in the manufacture of
paper and the printing process (kiss impression). Another thing Bodoni
and other ‘modern’ type casters did was to acknowledge the ‘true’ nature
of printing types, as distinct from written letters. I have already
mentioned that by the time of Baskerville and Bodoni, the angle of the nib
used to draw the letters had changed from 45° to a more upright position.
A closer look at Bodoni’s letters, however, reveals that this was not
strictly true. For instance while his d and F may follow this rule, the
letters N and W most certainly do not. You could argue that Bodoni
cheated, but actually, he was simply engraving (not writing) letters which
would be engraved as punches, and he followed the requirements of the
printer rather than the scribe, ensuring the letters both resembled

themselves and could be distinguished clearly from each other. A slavish
recreation of letters with the nib at a fixed angle would have produced a
page of dark vertical strokes as difficult to read as any Gothic. Meaning is
created through a pattern of similarity and difference. In a typeface, the
letters need to resemble each other enough to harmonise on the page,
whilst being different enough from each other to be identifiable.

This recognition of the needs of the print environment, however, was not
absent from type design prior to Bodoni’s time. Ashley argues that
Jenson’s creation of his roman depended very much on his understanding
of the differences between printing and writing.

Thus it could be argued that Jenson chose the Caroline minuscule for his
model, not just because it had been so recently revived in late 15th century
Venice, but because it was a style with few ligatures: ideal for the casting
of separate types which would be combined well together to form words:
something a scribe did naturally because they were always in the business
of drawing words, adjusting their shape to suit personal style and the
space available.
Still, however, the idea persists that each era deserves its own type, just as
earlier eras had their own styles of handwriting. Every generation of
typographers throws up examples of people willing to push the envelope
of letterforms, to argue for a new orthography, as we will discover shortly.

For much of the 20th century, the argument has been in favour of sans serif
types. In an age when most people, when they write, do so on a day-today basis with a ballpoint pen – an instrument which produces letters of
even stroke width with no contrast between thick and thin – it seems only
logical that the most ‘legible’ typeface will be one with even strokes and
no serifs. Why the scare quotes around ‘legible’? Because I am unhappy
with the term. In this instance, one could substitute the word ‘popular’
and make as much sense.
While the first ballpoint pen was patented by John H Loud in 1888, the
first sans serif types actually appeared in the early 19th century and (as
novelty types) were principally intended for the growing field of
advertising. They were used for newspaper headlines as well as for
advertising copy lines. As mentioned above, in any two-paper city, there
would be one using Bodoni-style ‘fat face’ display letters and one using

sans serif: otherwise known as Grotesques or grots, or (confusingly, by
Americans) even gothics31. The grots were slow to catch on, however, and
book printers especially continued to make use of ancient and reliable
types like Baskerville and Caslon32.
The pejorative use of the terms gothic and grotesque in describing sans
serif types is indicative of how ugly these unfamiliar types were
considered to be. Familiarity has bred out this contempt, however, and
most modern designers would probably express a preference for sans
serif. In fact, nine out of ten of the top sellers in the Linotype font library
are sans serif faces33. That said, an attractively drawn sans serif is a
relatively recent phenomenon. The 19th century ‘grots’ were indeed very
ugly.
The nineteenth century was an era of ethereal here-today, gone-tomorrow
novelty display types, whether they were ‘Egyptian’ slab-serifs, fat-face
Bodonis, highly decorated styles, or grots. Type was of more emphatic
importance in the 1900s because there was little scope for illustration. A
glance at the small ads in any provincial newspaper will demonstrate how
homogeneous a page of advertisements can be. The main thing was to
have a display type in your publicity spot that was at least slightly
different from all the other advertisements. In contrast to some of the
highly decorated display faces, it was only natural that there should be
plain, unadorned faces also. The problem sans serif designers often have,
however, is in maintaining the natural proportions and weights of the
letters whilst adhering to what are believed to be their essential forms.
Experiments have shown that people are remarkably adept at reading
information, even when much of it is elided. You can chop off the bottom
half of a line of type, for example, and most people would still be able to
tell you what it says.

In the example above, you can see that it’s easier to read text that has lost
its bottom half than it is to read text that has lost its top half. So in terms
of the essential forms of letters, it would seem that top trumps bottom,
which indicates that losing the serifs along the bottom of letters would be
trivial. But there’s a huge difference between letters in isolation and

letters forming words. If my example above was in an unfamiliar
language, it would be much harder to work out what it said. Language
information in writing contains far more than just the shapes of letters.
There’s a whole system of orthography, and the conventions of reading
and writing (left-to-right or right-to-left, word spacing, letter spacing).
The message that gets through may be simple, but it depends on a great
deal. There is, as Serres would argue, redundancy in the system.

While the concept of the essential forms of letters is
problematic (and will be explored further in Part
Three), letters do have natural proportions. An a is
an a because it is different from e and d.
The difficulty with a grot design is in knowing how much you can take
away from a letter and still know what it is – even in an unfamiliar word.
Indeed type compositors objected to some grots because of the difficulty
in distinguishing the reversed letterforms in the case. Digital typographers
don’t have to deal with this problem, because what you see is what you
get. In traditional printing, however, there were individuals who were
trained in reading whole pages of text in reverse, and printers setting type
by hand really did have to mind their ps and qs.

From our current perspective, it’s possible to identify three main styles in
sans serif design, all of which claim to produce type in its most ‘essential’
and legible form. The first style features type in humanist proportions
with variations of stroke width which still evoke the split nib of a quill or
fountain pen: effectively roman letters but without the serifs. A second
sans serif style features type designed without those variations of stroke
width – these are known as monolines. The third strand is related to the
monoline, but departs from it in seeking to design type with
mathematically calculated geometric proportions. In the text that follows,
I will refer to these styles as humanist, monoline, and geometric.

Monoline and geometric types are related to each other via the
enlightenment thinking I have previously mentioned. These types can be
characterised as ‘industrial’ in that they are designed to fit in with the
machine age. ‘Machine type’ should be suitable both for machine setting
(Linotype or Monotype) and high-speed printing. It should also reflect, in
that nebulous way, the industrial age which inspired it. The enlightenment
strand was always strongest in continental Europe, shifting from Germany
in the pre-1939 period to Switzerland after the war, when it became
known as the ‘International’ style.
In 1925, Herbert Bayer of the Bauhaus school in Germany drew a singlecase alphabet that remained as sketches until decades later. It didn’t have
upper and lower case, but combined elements of the two in one alphabet.
Bayer’s idea for this ‘universal’ typeface reflects the belief that letters
should be drawn ‘scientifically’, with geometric straights and curves, with
the shapes of the letters dictated by mathematical principles. As we’ll see,
this particular enlightenment idea was hardly new, though it was presented
as if it was. Bayer was also responsible for the standard Bauhaus
letterheads, which again were printed using letters of the lower case only.

The sketches for the universal typeface did not include any capital letters.
This ideological position of the Bauhaus was a reaction against that
peculiarity of the written German language, in which all nouns (not just
proper names) were capitalised. The Bauhaus saw the use of two
alphabets in typography (majuscules and minuscules) as irrational. In the

1990s, Bayer’s universal was turned into a digital font by a number of
type foundries, including P22:

Bayer’s sketches didn’t make the final production stage perhaps because
of another type created in parallel with the Bauhaus movement which
became much more successful — almost certainly because it wasn’t quite
so dogmatic and ideologically inspired. In 1927, Paul Renner, working for
the Bauer type foundry, released the first version of Futura, a typeface
which was inspired by pure geometric principles. Futura spawned a host
of imitators and remains popular over 85 years later. It is beautiful,
elegant, and versatile. It works well in all caps (its capitals are
fantastically sharp) as well as in lower case, and it’s used by artists and
commercial designers alike. Of all the types that attempted to burn down
the typographical house and rebuild it without rats in the foundations,
Futura seems to have been the most successful. But its beauty and utility
may stem from compromises within its design, which we’ll consider later.

Renner was friendly with another typographer. Jan Tschichold, who had
been converted to the possibilities of the Bauhaus approach after visiting a
1924 exhibition. In 1928, Tschichold published Die Neue Typographie,
which has proven to be a very influential book - eventually leading to the
ubiquitous Swiss/International style of typography. It’s especially
important in a German context because the conditions that prevailed there
– even in the early 20th century – were such that the old Fracturs were
considered to be national type. As late as 1930, almost 60 percent of
newly published books and nearly all newspapers were composed in
Fractur. In 1933, Hitler announced that the Fractur was the only
appropriate letterform for the German language: this meant that it was in
wider use in the 20th century than in earlier times34.

Tschichold was still in his 20s when he wrote Die Neue Typographie,
which was subtitled, ‘a handbook for modern designers.’ The bulk of his
handbook is concerned with such typographical esoterica as the
standardisation of paper sizes, and an extended tirade against what he
called ‘the old typography (1440—1914)’ . His main argument was for
layout designers to stop centring everything, and to seek asymmetry rather
than symmetry. He was interested in the ways in which information could
be displayed on the page in a hierarchical way, so that it was read in the
‘correct’ order. He was especially keen that the movements in the visual
arts – characterised by a series of ‘-isms35’ – which gave the impression of
progress should transfer across into typography, which he considered to
be stuck in a very deep rut. At one point, Tschichold quotes the
‘manifesto’ of the Italian Futurist poet, F T Marinetti in attacking the Arts
and Crafts style of design:
I am starting typographic revolution, directed above all against the
idiotic, sick-making conception of the old-fashioned poetry book,
with its handmade paper, it’s 16th century style, decorated with
galleons, Minervas, Apollos, great initials, flourishes and
mythological vegetables… The book must be the futuristic expression
of that futuristic thought. Better: my revolution is against among
other things the so-called typographic harmony of the page, which is
in complete opposition to the flow of style which the page allows. We
will, if need be, use three or four different colours and 20 different
typefaces on the same page. Four example, italic for a series of
similar and swift sensations, bold for the imitation of heavy tones
and so on36.

This ‘manifesto’ can be seen primarily in opposition to the contemporary
private presses which formed a part of the Arts and Crafts movement in
Britain and the United States. It bears the same relationship to traditional
printing as our contemporary campaigns against over-used type like
Helvetica, Comic Sans, and Papyrus. Traditional printing was seen by
these futurists as one big, stultifying cliché. Of course, the private presses
saw themselves in opposition to something, too: the degradation in
printing which resulted from industrial revolution. Tschichold and the
New Typographers were celebrating that revolution, demanding that the
appearance of the printed page should reflect the age in which it was
created. It is clear that both sides saw industrialisation as a form of
epistemological break. The private presses were busy revising forgotten
crafts: handset, old-style types, handmade papers; while the New
Typographers were insisting that if the type is modern, and the paper is
modern, then the modern printing process is better than the old one.
Marinetti’s manifesto proposes the special use of bold and italic types, as
if the use of different letterforms for special purposes was a new idea.
Ironically, Tschichold’s own argument was undermined by the printing of
his book. He was forced to use an anonymous nineteenth century grot as
the typeface for the book, because Renner’s Futura wasn’t ready in time.
The result is a poorly printed volume, with a typeface unsuitable for
setting long passages of prose. It’s too light, leaving the pages looking
faint and hard to read. It’s a stark contrast to Morris’s densely overdecorated, hand-made, “quoted” poetry books. As an example of book
design for a typography manifesto, it’s a failure – and put to shame by
Eric Gill’s Essay on Typography, for which Gill designed his own font.
In spite of the international aspects of Futurism, and of the Left with
which Tschichold was aligned politically, the importance of the German
context cannot be underestimated: in a country in which he was
surrounded by Fractur types, where even railway station signs were being
erected with Fractur lettering, Tschichold’s desire to see a cleaner, clearer
typography is understandable37. Another Aspect of the German New
Typographical Movement is that Peculiarity of the German Language
which sees all Nouns given capital Initials. It was this archaic Habit which
saw Tschichold argue for a rationalised Orthography.
His first argument was with the use of sans serif type:

None of the typefaces to whose basic form some kind of ornament has
been added (serifs in Roman type, lozenge shape and curlicues in
Fractur) meet our requirements for clarity and purity. Among all the
types that are available, the so-called “Grotesque” (sanserif) or
“block letter” (“skeleton letters” would be a better name) is the only

But Tschichold did not want simply to use the grots which were then
available. He believed that the ‘essential characteristics’ of type had yet to
be worked out:
…the lower-case letters are still too like their ‘humanistic’
counterparts … all the attempts up to now produce a type for our
time are merely ‘improvements’ on the previous sanserifs: they are
still too artistic, too artificial, in the old sense, to fulfil what we need
today. ¶ Personally, I believe that no single designer can produce the
typeface we need, which must be free from all personal
characteristics: it will be the work of a group, among whom I think

A ‘type for our time’ designed by a committee which includes an
engineer, and free from all personal characteristics. There’s something
revealing and faintly disturbing about Tschichold’s anti-humanist stance.
What does it mean to be anti-humanist? In the ferment of 20th century
politics, anti-humanist philosophy led towards communism or fascism.
Both political systems came to be seen not just as anti-humanist, but antihuman. While Marx had argued that humanistic talk of ‘rights’ was
simply part of the general repression of populations, the fake communists
in the Kremlin continued to repress. As for the fascists in Spain, Italy, and
Germany, they set out to control the totality of society, determined to
create efficient systems: designing type, building motorways, and making
the trains that took people to death camps run on time. The futurist hatred
of the ‘sick-making’, decorated poetry book, shades into a hatred of the
kind of people who produced them. A hatred of art, of poetry, a negative
view of human nature: all add up to a form of misanthropy.
In orthodox printing history, classic types seem always to have been
reported as the work of inspired visionaries: Jenson, Aldus, Granjon,
Baskerville, Bodoni. The truth is, however, that the creation of a type was
and is rarely the work of one man or woman. Type is one of those places
where art and engineering meet: both depend on each other. And before I

continue my discussion of Tschichold, I have to return to an idea I
mentioned in passing a little earlier.
Before I covered the developments in design represented by Baskerville
and Bodoni, I should have mentioned an important moment in type
history that took place in Paris in the late seventeenth century. Its proper
place in linear time was passed over, however, because it seemed more
appropriate to discuss it now.
In 1692, Louis XIV authorised the creation of a new series of types
for the exclusive use of the Imprimerie Royale… [T]he disciples of
Descartes and Mersenne were pressing forward a programme to
rationalise everything and, with the rest, the forms that record and
transmit thought… The committee charged by the Académie des
Sciences engaged in the construction of all the letters to be used in
Richelieu’s foundation upon a basis that might have astonished him.
The patterns for the punch-cutter to follow were engraved on copper
plates, each letter on a field divided into 2,305 squares40.
Here, over 200 years before the Bauhaus, was a type to be designed on
scientific principles (what could be more scientific than a 48x48 grid!) by
a committee. I doubt, therefore I think; I think, therefore I am redesigning
the alphabet. Burning the house down to rid it of rats: rebuilding the
house with a t-square, a compass and a ruler.
Work began on what are known as the Romains du Roi in 1692, and the
first proof was produced seven years later. Morison and Updike disagree
on the relative merits of the letters produced, which had horizontal,
unbracketed serifs, some slightly condensed characters and an increased
x-height with shorter extenders. In all, an extremely ‘modern’ type, as
might be expected of the Enlightenment. Morison plays the enlightenment
joker for all his worth:
Briefly, therefore, the Paris type of 1699–1702 presents the first of a
style that departed from the humanistic design that had dominated
the Roman [sic] letter since 1495. It set a new style, that of a
rationalist and economic design which went far to compromise the
accepted theoretical basis of typography41.
As mentioned above, however, Nicolas Jenson in 1470 had already
recognised that printed letters had to work differently together than those
in script. Also, as soon as Aldus changed the angle of the cross-bar in e

from Jenson’s with-the-angle-of-the-pen upward slant to an horizontal, the
engraved nature of type as opposed to the written nature of calligraphy
became clear. Jenson’s genius lay in working out designs for letters that
allowed them to harmonise without the need for the constant adjustments
possible in written scripts.

faces unpleasing. For this reason many artists prefer to use capitals
only, to avoid mixing them with lower case… From this one can see
that it is now recognised that the two alphabets of Roman are really
two different styles, and should be used in parallel, but not mixed.
The New Typography demands economy in type design. To redesign
our letters completely – as in shorthand and lettering for the Blind –
would be impractical and unacceptable. So we have to make do with
the type we have, the capitals and the lowercase. To decide which to

choose is not difficult, because capitals in continuous text are too
difficult to read43…
It’s odd to consider a typography which desires to use bold and italic for
special purposes, but wishes to eschew the use of capitals. Capitals are so
useful in marking the beginnings of sentences, the names of characters in
fiction, the names of cities and towns, or people in telephone directories.
To propose their abolition seems quite perverse (though of course the
German context to this argument must be borne in mind). With his
passion for radical change, and leftist associations in opposition to the
prevailing German political atmosphere, it was perhaps inevitable that
Tschichold would be arrested. When the National Socialists came to
power in 1933, Tschichold was taken into ‘protective custody’ as a
‘cultural Bolshevik’. He was held for six weeks and lost his job. His arrest
is a perfect encapsulation of what totalitarianism really means: Hitler and
his Nazi party sought to control everything, down to the national typeface.
On his release, the type heretic Tschichold took refuge in Basel,
Switzerland, where he remained – apart from a brief stay in England after
the war – until his death in 1974. Ironically, his experiences under Hitler
made him change his position on type. By the mid-1930s, he had decided
that the design principles he had promoted in Die Neue Typographie
demonstrated ‘shocking parallels with the teachings of National Socialism
and Fascism44.’ In 1935, he published Typographische Gestaltung,
referring to some of his own earlier statements. In 1967, he tried to revise
Die Neue Typographie for a new edition, but no publisher could be found.
He said at the time, ‘So many things in that primer were erroneous,
because my experience was too small45.’
The experience of the holocaust, too, must have been salutary. The point
about the Nazis declaring the Fractur to be the German national type was
not that they were passionate about alphabets, but that in a totalitarian
society, everything is controlled. Those who try to build ‘rational’ systems
have a fear of disarray in all things. This was as true of The Sun King as it
was of Hitler. Why else form a committee to burn down a perfectly good
house to rid it of rats? Because you fear the rat in yourself:
How many of these rough political rats are there around us? How
many of them break things they don’t understand? How many of

these rats simplify? How many of them have built such
homogeneous, cruel systems upon the horror of disorder and noise46?
Meanwhile, to prove the point about totalitarianism, in 1940 Hitler
realised that world domination would be difficult if people weren’t able to
read his propaganda, so he declared that roman would from now on be the
German standard, as Fractur was a ‘Schwabacher-Jewish type47’. (It’s not
enough that it’s unreadable – the typeface’s national identity must be
erased with an Orwellian racist slur.) The difference between Tschichold
and the Nazis was that the son of the signwriter actually cared about
letterforms and design.
Tschichold did still show some totalitarian tendencies in the 1940s, when
he was hired by Penguin books in London as their chief designer.
Tschichold harmonised the design of Penguin’s paperbacks, publishing
guidelines for consistency. Most interesting was the typeface he chose for
the setting of the text inside the books: Plantin. Christophe Plantin had
been a Dutch printer based in Antwerp, and his type was influenced by
the earlier Granjon designs used by Claude Garamont in Paris. In another
ironic twist, when Tschichold designed the covers for a series of
Shakespeare, he used Eric Gill’s Perpetua type – another humanist design
which was a long way from Futura:

Tschichold’s trajectory of opinion is fascinating and revealing of the
principles which have driven the development of print and typography.
What he and the other followers of Bauhaus ideology and promoters of
sans serif types were driving at was that the first principle of typography
is legibility. The truth is, however, that sometimes typographers get
caught up in the storage of information and the overall look of a page,
losing sight of the communication itself. The primary task of the

typesetter has always been to allow the reader access to an author’s
thoughts without interference. If a reader notices the typeface, so the
argument goes, then the typographer has failed. The ‘rational’ and
‘logical’ end to the legibility argument always seems to be that a pure,
unadulterated letterform would be easier to read, and more conducive to
clear communication, than the old-fashioned serifed roman typefaces,
especially in the smaller point sizes demanded by economics. To decide to
ditch capital letters simply because – to an expert in letterforms – they are
a ‘completely different style’ is to forget that the difference is the whole
point when it comes to communication.

helvetica and the international style
Although he abandoned his own early ideas, Tschichold and the Bauhaus
school were very influential on what came to be known as the Swiss or
International style of typography, which came to prominence in the postwar era, and has gone on to dominate the world of graphic design since
the 1950s. It’s an irony that one of the the most popular typefaces in this
movement outside Switzerland, Helvetica, doesn’t really suit the style.
Although it is named for Switzerland (Helvetica being the Latin name for
Switzerland), Helvetica itself is neither 1950s-modern in its design nor
particularly “Swiss” in its approach to letterforms.
Originally called Neue Haas Grotesk, it was based on an 1896 German
design called Akzidenz Grotesk. As such, it has a very 19th-century feel,
and is far less suitable for the “International” style than other fonts such as
Univers, Frutiger, and Eurostile.

In fact, Helvetica was never really part of the Swiss school of typography,
and its current popularity is almost entirely down to clever marketing: it
was included with Linotype machines in the USA, and consequently

included with every Macintosh computer. Thus a phenomenon was born
(watch the documentary to learn more48). Here’s Bruno Maag on the
subject:

“That’s the point, it is vanilla ice cream. In my
whole career in typography, starting with my
apprenticeship, I have never used Helvetica. Being a
Swiss typographer, it’s always been Univers. Even
in my apprenticeship we didn't have Helvetica in the
printshop. Then I went to Basel school of design
and of course in Weingart’s workshop it was
Univers, never Helvetica. Then I come to England
and there’s all these designers using Helvetica! The
Macintosh had just come out and Helvetica was on
every single machine. Everyone was so fascinated
with it … I never understood that49.

These “Swiss” types straddle the line between humanist and geometric
sans serif letterforms. Their chief characteristic is that there is a general
lack of contrast between thick and thin strokes (they’re sometimes called
monolines for that reason). The design philosophy of the international
style was to keep the page clean (lots of white space), to avoid justified
text, and to maintain legibility by not drawing attention to the type. The

type is meant to be completely neutral, anonymous. This is especially
evident with Univers, the successor to Bayer’s more radical “universal”
typeface, which is meant to be a type without any personality. It is
therefore “universal” in its application, and can be combined with any
other style of type quite successfully. The International Style is really a
design school very suited to signage: road signs, airports, hospitals. The
conflict arrives when the style is considered for articles and books: blocks
of text meant for sustained reading and concentration. It’s still very rare to
find a whole book set in a sans serif, unless it’s some kind of large-format
“coffee-table” art book.
As I mentioned above, and as was clear from some of the opinions quoted
from Die Neue Typographie, the ‘machine’ types designed on geometric
principles were not the only game in town when it came to sans serifs.
There was in fact a very strong ‘humanist’ movement. These Swiss
designs have some humanist characteristics (the two-story a, and there is
still some variation in stroke width) but are designed without serifs.
The basic credo of ‘humanist’ type design is that the letters should have
the appearance of those made by the human hand, rather than the
mechanical appearance of the more geometric or ‘scientific’ types. They
should, to quote Alexander Lawson, be ‘both beautiful and utilitarian50’.
The problem with the machine types was that they could never quite live
up to their publicity. The compromises made in their rendering always
suggested the truth that letters are not simply geometric shapes that can be
drawn with a line of fixed width, but always contained the variation and
innovation that the human hand naturally creates. For example, the letter a
of Futura should, according to geometric principles, consist of a circle
attached to a line. To prevent the distortion that would result from the
thickening at the point where the circle and line intersect, however, a
compromise was made in its execution:

I hinted earlier that the success of Futura was attributable to the
compromises in its design. To the totalitarian way of thinking, the very
word compromise hints at fatality and failure, but that’s a problem with
ideology and not a typeface. Futura is a perfect example of art meeting
engineering. It’s both elegant and functional, beloved of artists such as
Barbara Kruger and graphic designers in commercial magazines. Futura’s
failure is its success. It works because it does not work. Given that the
project of producing a scientific, geometric type was thus ‘fatally’
compromised, the humanist designers set about producing their own sans
serifs. Let’s consider two of the most well-known.

Optima
Optima is interesting because it was designed by German calligrapher and
type designer Hermann Zapf. As a humanist sans serif, it goes against the
German typographical traditions outlined above. Zapf is renowned for
typefaces like Palatino (1948), his calligraphic fonts Chancery and
Zapfino, and the ubiquitous collection of graphic symbols, Zapf Dingbats.
In a German context, Zapf’s championing of humanist type isolated him
against an anti-humanist ideological backdrop, the two sides fighting over
geometrical and fractur types, leaving Zapf looking like a brave, lone,
hero: a Galileo of type up against the totalitarian Church.

Galileo’s tomb in the Santa Croce. Photo Michael Jiroch51

The fascinating thing about Optima is that it’s a sans serif font based on a
model dating back hundreds of years. It gives the lie to the idea that the
sans serif is a phenomenon of modernity and industrialisation. Unusually
for a sans serif, Optima shows a high degree of stroke variation, with a
slight swelling at the terminals of the strokes that almost suggests a serif.
The model Zapf used was, fittingly, the inscriptional lettering on the tomb
of Galileo Galilei in the Basilica of Santa Croce in Florence, Italy. Galileo
died in 1642, but the inscription looks far more modern because of its
sans serif design.

Gill Sans
In a British context, perhaps the most famous and widely used of the
humanist sans serifs was Eric Gill’s design, which was originally known
as Monotype Sans Serif. Gill Sans is now available in a wide variety of
weights and widths, some of which depart quite considerably from the
original vision. Gill Sans is instantly recognisable as the corporate identity
of the BBC and the font used in the ubiquitous “Keep Calm and Carry
On” poster and other souvenir items:

The Keep Calm poster was just one of several produced by the Ministry of Information at the
outbreak of World War 2 in 1939

Gill’s design was commissioned by Monotype to counter the commercial
threat of Paul Renner’s Futura. It was influenced by calligrapher Edward
Johnston and the corporate typeface he produced for the London
Underground (with assistance from Gill) between 1913 and 1916. Gill’s
type is perhaps too self-consciously different to Johnston’s Underground,
and contains many questionable letter designs. But these all add to its odd
charm. The blacker fonts within the typeface look like a completely
different set of letters and bear almost no relationship to the light or
medium versions. The end result is something quintessentially British: a
long way from being efficient, it positively shouts, this is how we do
things round here. Gill Sans was immediately taken up by railway
companies for poster designs, and was also used to print timetables. One
key advantage of a sans serif design is that it will work at the extremely
small sizes used in time-tables and schedules. It works brilliantly as an
uppercase for poster designs, but its lower case is probably too odd to be
used for extended passages of text.

Like Tschichold, Eric Gill published his own typographical manifesto, in
the form of his An Essay on Typography (1931). Reading both books, you
can almost hear the conversational debate between the two men. Gill was
involved with the Arts and Crafts movement, lived in a rural socialist
community, and was also responsible for the stations of the cross carvings

in Westminster Cathedral and sculptures at BBC Broadcasting House52.
Given the disdain Tschichold expressed for the arts and crafts crowd, Gill
might be expected to take an opposite stance, but some of his ravings
about people make him seem positively anti-humanist. From his first
chapter, it’s clear that he and Tschichold have much in common, but
different approaches to solving the same problem.

The characteristic differences between the arts and crafts background of
Gill and the futurist ideas of new wave typography would generally place
Gill in the renaissance camp, and Tschichold in the enlightenment camp.
But in his first chapter, Gill sets out his Orwellian vision of England in
1931:
The abnormality of our time, that which makes it contrary to nature,
is its deliberate & stated determination to make the working life of
men and the product of their working hours mechanically perfect, &
to relegate all the humanities, all that is of its nature humane, to
their spare time, to the time when they are not at work53.
In other words, Gill – like the whole arts and crafts movement – was
concerned with the alienation of labour, and the way that industrial
capitalism had destroyed craftsmanship. Gill is approaching typography
from a Marxian perspective. He is precisely aligned with Tschichold as a
left-aligned typographer with anti-humanist feelings. To both, Humanism
is just a comforting illusion, the Emperor’s same-old clothes, concealing
the repression of the working classes by the ruling class. As such, Gill
wants to strip away the decoration, revealing the true nature of
Capitalism. Early in his essay, he is concerned to see industrial
typography revealed in its true nature, for the two worlds of work and
leisure, industrialism and craft, to be rigidly demarcated54. A machineprinted page should look like itself, not disguise itself as craftsmanship.
Unlike Tschichold, however, Gill foresaw that the least adorned lettering

would come from an artist, not an engineer. This is their major point of
difference:
Fancy lettering will be as distasteful to the artist as it will be into the
engineer – in fact it is more than probable that it will be the artists
who will give the lead. It has always been so. It is not the artist who
is sentimental – it is the men of business and the man of science.
Even now there are very few really logical and relentless alphabets
of plain letters in common commercial use in this country, and they
were designed by artists55.
Like his contemporaries Morison and Tschichold, and like his
predecessors Grandjean, Fournier, Baskerville and Bodoni, Gill was
concerned that printing types should be of their time, and not slavish
limitations of an earlier age. Especially with machine composition, Gill
was dead against the overlaying of fake ‘humane’ elements. In spite of
himself, however, the alphabets he drew, which were struck as types by
Monotype, have all the elegance and artistry of a brilliant craftsman. Gill
Sans is so far from being perfect, it’s positively quirky with personality.
Gill’s roman alphabets, like Perpetua, Joanna, and their respective italics,
all bear the hallmarks of his individual style and the ‘personal
characteristics’ so hated by the futurists. In fact, I would go so far as to
say that nobody in the history of typography has ever drawn italic like
Gill. Referring again to Serres, it’s clear that while Gill thought that he
was burning down the house in order to build it free from noisy rats in the
foundations, these rats are always-already there, and his resulting types
are unmistakably his, carrying evidence of his all-too-human ‘mistakes’.

Not only do Gill’s fonts all have a distinctive quality to the trained eye
(for instance, his lower-case d is always very quirky), but they also bear a
strong resemblance – to the untrained eye – to the letterforms of earlier

eras. Indeed, the Perpetua capitals could be mistaken for authentic Roman
inscriptional capitals. It is testament to the Janus-faced nature of type that
Gill’s designs can appear to be generic even as they are uniquely quirky.
Another interesting aspect of Gill’s work is the fact that his display letters,
his romans, and his sans serifs all blend well together, so that when used
together they complement one another, allowing the information on the
page to be displayed clearly and economically. In this, Gill’s achievement
recalls those scribes at Tours who used three different letter styles in their
books to denote different kinds of material and levels of importance.
While it had been traditional since the time of Garamond to combine a
roman with an italic, and in later eras to use different weights and sizes,
Gill was the first typographer to achieve a successful blend of
complementary sans serif and serif letters, something that has become
much more common in the age of digital type foundries.

revolutions
But while Gill, in spite of his Arts and Crafts and allegiances, may have
created a perfect set of printing types for the age of mechanical
composition, he perhaps recognised his failure to break with the past in
lettering, as he wished to do. The revival of which he formed a part was of
Humanist principles rather than ancient letterforms. In concluding his
Essay, he asks, ‘But Why Lettering?’
It is not only in the matter of spelling that letters are ridiculous… not
only have letters largely ceased to signify the sounds of our
language, but the business of writing bears no relation to the
business of speaking… Writing is not written talk; it is a translation
of talk into a clumsy & difficult medium which has no relation
whatever to the time factor of speech & very little relation to the
sound. It is an entirely outworn, decayed & corrupt convention
whose chief & most conspicuous character is its monumental witness
to the laziness and irrationality of men & women56.
Here Gill echoes all logocentrists since Plato, tracing ‘the sense of being
to the logos, to discourse the reason… and [considering] writing or
technique to be secondary to logos57.’ Whereas Tschichold three years
earlier merely flirted with the idea of reforming orthography altogether,

mentioning that a move to shorthand would be impracticable and going on
to propose reform in the shape of the abolition of capitals, Gill demands a
total revolution:
What is the revolution demanded? Reformed spelling? No, the
abolition of spelling—the abolition of lettering as we know it
altogether… For what is the remedy? It is plain before our eyes.
Nothing is required but that every child shall be taught ‘short-hand’
at school… ¶But let us abolish the word shorthand— let us call it
phonography or even simply writing58.
It seems extraordinary (not to mention perverse) that a man who loved
letters as much as Gill obviously did should propose such a thing, but then
it is in keeping with an anti-humanist stance, an attitude revealed clearly
by the reference to ‘laziness and irrationality’ above. But a move to
shorthand would take us back to the days when handwriting was only
legible to the person to whom it belonged. Shorthand represents a speedy
and compact method of storage, but a poor means of communication. The
view that sees writing as storage is also the one that sees writing as a
supplement to speech. I believe, however, that it is simply in the nature of
those closely involved with print and the problem of letterforms to want
to innovate and meddle with an alphabet that was inherited from very
ancient ancestors indeed. Such people are always involved with the very
latest in communications technology, and often possess an innovative
nature. For we inherited to cogito, also, and the need to doubt and
question the validity of what went before this year’s model of the
Copernican Revolution. Copernicanism is, after all, just another form of
centrism, placing Reason at the centre of the Universe, and consigning
everything in the past to the irrational. Epistemological ruptures always
make us ‘righter than was ever possible before59’:
¶Let me say a word on the idea of progress. We conceive of time as
an irreversible line, whether interrupted or continuous, of
acquisition and inventions. We go from generalisations to
discoveries, leaving behind us a trail of errors finally corrected…
Whew! We’ve finally arrived at the truth… ¶…I cannot help thinking
that this idea is the equivalent of those ancient diagrams we laugh at
today, which place the earth at the centre of everything, our galaxy
at the middle of the universe, to satisfy our narcissism60.

While Derrida traces logocentrism back to Plato, Serres identifies the
centre of this particular kind of centrism – ‘science’s bid to take over the
totality of reason,’ – as the eighteenth century of the Enlightenment and
the Romaine du Roi. Those areas consigned to the irrational included not
just history and the past, but religion, literature and the humanities. But he
goes on to point his finger at the ‘renaissance’ mind-set also:
The converse prejudice is no more enlightening though – claiming
that we have totally forgotten and initial intuition received and
developed only by certain pre-Socratics among the ancient Greeks61.
New technology continues to throw up examples of temporal centrism.
Helvetica-hater Bruno Maag is given to pondering the future of type in
the age of the electronic book, and his views resemble Gill’s in an
uncanny way:
I envisage a move away from the alphabet as we know it towards a
more pictorial approach. One that maybe is not based any more on
individual letters forming the word, but on word pictures. This may
seem heretical to some people but I believe that we ought to have
open minds. The next generation, which now is growing up with
Nintendo and is used to a different way of communicating, will very
quickly adapt to some new system. Letterforms will change62.
The Egyptians drew hieroglyphs on papyrus with a brush; the scribes at
Tours used a goose quill to form their minuscules; Jenson engraved
punches hundreds of years later to cut his first roman; Bodoni gave his
types over entirely to the idea that they were engraved, not written;
Tschichold believed that a naked sans serif was the only appropriate type
for the machine age; Gill proposed that the Carolingian letters should be
abolished altogether. In 1996, Bruno Maag proposed that computer ‘type’
be reformed so that we are again drawing pictures-for-words, just like the
ancient Egyptians.
There’s something about this continual need to tinker, this circular
argument about type that ‘fits the spirit of the age’. I’ve spent a lot of time
discussing the renaissance desire to revive the perfection of the past and
the enlightenment impulse to invent something new. Is there a way to
understand letters and leave these concepts aside?

The Naked Letter and the Parasite
the parasite
MICHEL SERRES’ BOOK The Parasite plays with that word’s triple meaning
in the French language1.The first meaning is the most familiar to English
speakers: a biological organism which lives inside or upon its ‘host’. The
second meaning is also fairly familiar: a social parasite, one who accepts
hospitality (or lives off society) but gives nothing in return. This second
meaning could be viewed as a metaphorical play on the first, except for
the fact that the basic vocabulary of the first meaning (guest/host)
predates the science, indicating that the metaphorical relationship is the
other way round. The third meaning is more obscure: interference in
communication, static, noise.
In this somewhat fuzzy spot, a parasite is an abusive guest, an
unavoidable animal, a break in a message2.

Using illustrations from literature (most particularly Fables de la
Fontaine, in which, he says, all the animals behave like parasites), Serres
proposes the idea that systems can be seen as chains of parasites, with
each element of the chain both interrupting and feeding off the one before.
By ‘system’ Serres means not only social systems but information
systems. The two are closely related in his mind, because he has believed
since the late 1950s, ‘that we were reaching a culture, or society, in which
communication would hold precedence over production3.’ A study of
information or communication systems is concerned with what is carried
and how; the lines, paths, stations, borders, edges and forms; accidents in
the flow, changes, transformations and interruptions.

What passes might be a message, but parasites (static) prevent it
from being heard, and sometimes from being sent. Like a hole in a
canal that makes the water spill… there are escapes and losses,
obstacles and opacities. Doors and windows close… You don’t need
much experience to know that goods do not always arrive so easily
at their destination4…
In communications, the existence of this chain of parasites means that the
message cannot be transmitted without noise because, without the noise,
there is no message. The information is carried upon a wave of noise: the
parasite carries the message, and interferes with it at the same time. It
gives and takes, fluctuates, oscillates. There is background noise, which is
essential, and static, which is accidental. The signal-to-noise ratio cannot
be reduced to 1:0 without eliding the signal altogether. This idea works
for print communications as well as electronic. If part of a line of type is
obscured (as in the example in Part Two), the ‘signal’ still gets through
thanks to the redundancy in the system – which includes noise.
Back in the days of magnetic tape, the Dolby™ system of noise reduction
common to consumer electronics was notorious for removing some of the
‘brightness’ of a recording when removing the undesirable tape hiss. In
the end, you had to live with a certain amount of hiss5. In a final irony,
when digital recording systems were introduced (Pro Tools, for example),
one of the most common things a recording engineer would do was to
restore some of the noise/failures of the system by using software ‘plugins’ that emulated the frequency response of systems6. This would
include mixing consoles, pre-amplifiers, tape machines, and so on. There
are certain types of harmonic distortion which are actually quite ‘musical’
and pleasing to the ear. This is also true for an instrument like an electric
guitar played through an amplifier. Most professional guitarists still play
through a tube amp – even if they keep their sound ‘clean’ and relatively
undistorted. That’s because the underlying harmonic distortions and
frequency responses of the valves in the amplifier are still adding to the
sound. The software plug-ins that mathematically model such distortions
don’t come cheap, and the prized sounds usually use algorithms to
emulate the sound of equipment dating back to the 1950–1970 era, when
music actually did just sound better7.
Another common habit in the early digital days was to wait until the last
possible moment before converting analogue sounds to digital. Some

artists still insist on using magnetic tape – or would if they could afford it.
Analogue is a more expensive way of making recordings, but tape adds
‘warmth’ to the sound. ‘Warmth’ is an amorphous concept, usually
involving some kind of harmonic distortion, but people think they know it
when they hear it8. At the consumer end, many of those who saw the
introduction of the CD in 1983 as the ‘end’ of the vinyl LP may be
surprised to find that in 2013 the vinyl LP is not only still around but is
even growing in popularity among a generation who were born long after
its supposed end9.
This fad for ‘analogue’ extends to type, with both ‘distressed’ type and
type-that-looks-more-human remaining popular, two hundred years after
the industrial revolution. Even the dented prints of letterpress have made a
comeback – just check your local greetings card shop, or look at the
variety of prints available through such services as Etsy and even Apple10.
…[W]e know of no system that functions perfectly, that is to say,
without losses, flights, wear and tear, errors, accidents, opacity — a
system whose return is one for one… Everything happens as if the
following proposition were true: it works because it does not work…
[T]he rationalists of the generation before my own had the same
relation to Reason as old bigots have to virtue. It was more morality
than research… I think it was a certain relation with cleanliness; but
where do we put the dirt?11
Serres hints here at an aversion towards noise displayed by ultrarationalists. Think back to Tschichold’s Die Neue Typographie: his
aversion to the ‘unnecessary excrescences’ of serifs on letterforms is akin
to a certain kind of bigotry, as Tschichold himself acknowledged later,
when he compared his own earlier thinking to that of the Nazis. The
proverbial totalitarian efficiency (‘making the trains run on time’) is in
opposition to and undermined by Serres’ proposition:

It works because it does not work.
In communication, the ever-present background noise and the risk of a
lost signal is conquered by adding redundancies to the system. Each
additional redundancy, however, increases the risk of adding more
accidental noise and fatally obscuring the original signal. There is a fine

balance to be struck between information and noise, between the
exigencies of chance and programmed needs. Increasing the noise draws
attention to the carrier part of the message, which itself becomes the
message: interference.
Serres’ first illustration of a chain of parasites is the fable of ‘The Town
Rat and the Country Rat’. The country rat visits his cousin in the town;
the cousin lives in the house of a tax farmer12. After the tax collector has
finished his evening meal, the two rats begin to eat the leftovers and
crumbs. But they hear a sound, and because the country rat is terrified,
they run away, their meal incomplete. Serres analyses the meal as follows.
The tax farmer is a parasite upon the other farmers; he lives off their
production. The town rat is a parasite on the tax farmer, living on his
leftovers; the country rat is another parasite, a guest who gives nothing in
return for his meal, except perhaps conversation. The conversation and
the meal are interrupted by the noise (parasite in its third meaning). The
country rat has a horror of noise, because, argues Serres:
The country rat is the interrupter: he only trusts simple systems: he is
a rationalist13.
The last parasite (noise) argues Serres, temporarily stops the system,
bringing it full circle, because it could be the sound of the tax farmer
returning, altered by the scurrying of the rats in the night. Actually, of
course, this is not a full circle, because the farmers who produced the food
are not included. But this too is in the nature of the system, where there is
only ever a fleeting moment of stability in the overall flow of
goods/information.
Moments of interference/disruption in a system are points where it
changes direction, where information becomes static, and vice-versa. The
farmer interrupts the rats and temporarily halts the flow. A parasitic
system is normally all one-way traffic: the arrow of time and entropy,
‘that irreversible element which pushes the system toward death at
maximum speed14’. In this one-way street of communication, redundancies
in the system are progressively lost, and information drifts into
background noise, like the words drifting off the top of the tower of Babel
in Furnival’s concrete poem. At the points of interruption, however, where
two elements of the system meet, noise is instead transformed into
information, and ‘negentropy goes back upstream’:

For a moment the temporal sheaf makes a full circle. It forms a
turbulence where opposing times converge15.
In the flow of water in a stream, there are eddies in the current, moments
of turbulence, where the flow reverses. Because these moments of
turbulence are always present, they are in some sense stable. At such
points, the system forms islands of ‘fleeting stability’. Serres
conceptualises such systems as homeorhetic: always the same, always
different; a confluence of fluxes.
In Part Two, I referred to Serres’ views on writing as a system of both
storing and transmitting information. I would like now to return briefly to
this area before returning once more to typography. As mentioned
previously, Serres conceptualises written communication as the ‘battle of
the scribe and the reader, joined together by interest16’. The interest that
unites them is the eternal battle against the parasite: noise. On his part, the
scribe needs to ‘execute his drawing as well as possible’ and – most
importantly – the reader needs to recognise that although handwriting
may vary, and though the forms of the letters may not always be up to
standard, a badly executed drawing of a word still stands for that word.
Pathology of communication is not only an act of writing. It also
exists in spoken languages: stammerings, mispronunciations,
regional accents, dysphonias, and cacophonies. Likewise in the
technical means of communication: background noise, jamming,
static, cuts offs , hysteresis, various interruptions. ¶…[C]acography
is the noise of graphic form or, rather, the latter comprises an
essential form and a noise that is either essential or occasional. ¶…
[S]imply to write is to risk jumbling a form. In the same way, to
communicate orally is to risk losing meaning in noise17.
Because there is the same risk of noise or jumble in both written and oral
communication, Eric Gill was wrong to compare writing unfavourably
with speech18. Both forms of communication are subject to the same kinds
of interferences. Gill argues that speech is faster than writing, but takes no
account of the various accidents that can befall the spoken word:
spoonerisms, stutterings, misheard words fading on the wind or into the
background in a crowded room; - and, when technological means are used
to broadcast: feedback, power failures, distortions.

Michel Serres has admitted that the trajectory of his thinking has carried
him quickly through a myriad of disciplines, often leaving behind areas
which can be expanded upon19. I believe that typography is one such area,
for in setting up writing-as-cacography, Serres opposes it to printing with
his third footnote on page 66:
It is hardly necessary to add that the first benefit of the printing press
consists in permitting the reader not to be an epigraphist20.
It feels odd to be writing 30,000+ words in response to a footnote, but I
hope that the extended discussion of Part Two of the evolution of printed
letterforms, and the eternal search for the ‘ideal’ combination of legibility
and economy has demonstrated that this footnote represents a far-too-easy
dismissal. If communication systems are chains of parasites, if all such
systems are subject to noise, then there is interference in printing as
elsewhere, and the principles of good typography set out in Part One of
my discussion can be seen as part of the battle against noise, the
pathology of printed communication. The reason that the choice and size
of the type, the length of the line, the width of the margin, the line- and
word-spacing matter is precisely because these are all possible pitfalls,
moments when the physical presence of the words on the page can make
itself felt. As easily as a listener can become distracted by an accent, a
stutter, a rasp in the throat, a reader can lose the thread of an argument or
narrative plot and have to start over, or give up.
I want to think without an error, communicate without a parasite. So
I set the house on fire, the house of my ancestors. Done correctly, I
rebuild it without a rat. But … I must work without sleeping, without
turning my back, without leaving for a moment, without eating, But
at night, the rats return to the foundation… They are, as the saying
goes, always already there. Part of the building. Mistakes, wavy
lines, confusion, obscurity are part of knowledge; noise is part of
communication, part of the house21.
Writing depicts language in its concrete form; it represents the empirical
over the abstract. The problem that writing represents for rationality is
that I have already discussed: cacography. For language to appear in a
written form, there have to be standard signs for standard sounds; there
has to be agreement in its form, or the reader and the scribe (or printer)
would not be able to join together in the battle against noise. Clearly,
however, it is impossible for a rigorous standard to exist when every

written style is different. To get around this problem, we collectively
agree that an approximation of form is acceptable: a fuzzy standard.
Similarly, if I draw a rough square or circle, it is understood that I mean
those abstract ideas ‘a square’ and ‘a circle’ in the same way that I do
when I draw with a compass and rule. But still, my compass may be bent,
the pencil may slip, rain may drop on my page, or the cat walk across it: I
still have to accept inexactness, the fuzzy logic of the symbol:

…it is one and the same act to recognise an abstract being through the occurrences of its
concrete, standardised form and to come to an agreement about this recognition. In other
words, the act of eliminating cacography; the attempt to eliminate noise, is at the same time a
condition of the apprehension of the abstract form and the condition of the success of
communication22.

anexactitude
Letters are anexact forms; they exist as ideals only in the abstract.
Empirically, they can only ever approximate the ideal. Our language
arises from our perceptions, which can be infinitely tuned, so that we
could perceive every object differently. If we did not possess this idea of
the particular standing for the multiple, we would have to have a different
word for everything: every apple, every table, ‘every circle, every
symbol23.’
Reason has traditionally engaged in a battle against Chaos rather than
seeking to understand it. Recent decades have seen scientists begin to
grapple with chaotic systems, attempting to draw out the repeating
patterns and islands of stability in the flux. But all along, this has been the
way communication works, and especially written communication.
Writing represents and island of stability, with meaning arising out of the
background noise, sometimes fluctuating with ambiguity, sometimes
drifting into silence or static. The noise in letterforms, ike the rat in the

foundations, is always already there. The system works because it does
not work.
Those who have sought the ideal letterform, the naked letter, the one to
end all argument, have been seeking that Universal Science which would
include everything. It is understandable that in fighting the darkness of
chaos and ignorance, in seeking meaning against the background of
interference, they have wanted to eliminate all that is inessential.
At the extreme limits of empiricism, meaning is totally plunged into
noise, the space of communication is granular, dialogue is
condemned to cacophony: the transmission of communication is
chronic transformation. Thus, the empirical is strictly essential and
accidental noise24.
But the the search has been misguided, because there can be no such thing
as the essential letterform. the very edifice of scientific reason rests upon
the foundation that we can recognise the many in the particular. We name
categories of things rather than individual things themselves. As Derrida
points out, to imagine that there might have been, at one time, a unique
sign for each unique thing is ‘contradictory to the very concept and
operation of the sign.’
To determine the first sign in this way to found or deduce the entire
system of signs with reference to a sign which does not belong to that
system, is to reduce signification to presence25.
Without that unique human faculty of making meaning and pattern arise
from the background chaos, there would be no communication. So: there
have been many letterforms. If there had been just the one, apart from the
loss of a half-millennium of creative typography, we would still be with
Adam in Eden, giving names to all the plants and animals, one by one by
one. Which is to say, there would be no such thing as language as we
know it, no substitution and representation. Multiplicity is the only
‘essential’ element. The naked letter belongs with the unicorn. Language
is not the one that existed prior to the building of the tower of Babel, but
that chaotic entity that comes into existence in the moment of its fall.
There is still more to be said about typography. Its basic rules, as
discussed in Part One, are designed to arrange the printed matter on the
page so as to make reading as comfortable as possible, and to separate the
colour of the page from the surrounding background colours. The last

element of the typographical principles has been the most controversial:
the appropriate choice of typeface for the job. As we have seen in Part
Two, this can mean anything from the most legible, to the most striking, to
a typeface to reflect contemporary culture: legibility, suitability, and the
zeitgeist.
The question of legibility has tended to coincide with the quest for the
‘ideal’ letterform, with the 20th and 21st centuries in particular being the
era of grots or sans serifs – because it has been felt that the sans serif
types most reflected contemporary culture. (One reason for the current
fashion was alluded to earlier: with digital type on screen, the compositor
no longer needs to identify letterforms in their mirror images as individual
types.) A sans serif letter is one which has had all extraneous detail
stripped away: in particular, those parasitical serifs .There has been no
agreement, however, as to what exactly is left when the parasite has been
removed:

Further, it has never been established with any certainty whether a grot is
actually easier to read (i.e. more legible) than a conventionally serifed
roman. Sans serif can, however, actually be harder to read in certain
conditions. The English translation of Tschichold’s Die Neue Typographie
has been reproduced as a facsimile of the 1928 edition, and it has several
flaws. Firstly, because of the number of illustrations included, the paper
used is coated and shiny, making it a book both heavy to hold and difficult

to read in poor lighting conditions because of reflections. Secondly, the
sans serif chosen is Imagio Light Extended in just 7.75 point size with
11.6 point leading. Not only is the face small on the page, but it is
extremely light in colour, and its extended style means that there is much
space between letters as well as words. The overall effect is of too much
white space, and the book is a strain to read for long periods. These
conditions are hardly likely to endear a typeface to the general reader
looking for a relaxing read.

Burt (1958) concluded that people preferred to read a type with which
they were familiar, but did not even include a single sans serif in his
study. According to Ole Lund Burt’s work was seriously flawed for other
reasons, too, and amounts to little more than an assertion of opinion,
which is often the case when science and reason are brought in to end
arguments about type26. Burt is not alone in his opinion, however, and this
‘hypothesis of habit’ has much to recommend it, in that most readers of
books read nothing but serifed letters; but in an age when a significant
number of people do not read books, and fewer are reading any printed
matter at all, there is an argument to be made about what exactly is the
most familiar form of type. Those who read exclusively on screens or in
glossy magazines may indeed be perfectly comfortable with a sans serif.
They have now tried Green Eggs and Ham, and may even like it.
One of the assertions made about serifs is that they help letters to blend
together to form words. While some people claim to hear a voice in their
head when they read, announcing each word in turn, others absorb
information in blocks of four words at a time. Writing in this sense is
pictures of words, and the argument is that the “noise” of the serifs creates
a fuzzy blending of the letters. In support of this is the example of partconcealed type I used earlier. We really can work out what something says
even when quite a lot of it is hidden from view, because we can infer what
word-picture it is, even when we can’t see the individual letters clearly.
There is a fine distinction between Updike’s criticism of the Romain du
Roi on the grounds that the serifs give the page a ruled appearance, and
the simple blurring that the more usual serifs achieve. On the other hand,
you could argue that the ruling effect created by serifs allows the eye to

follow along the line more easily without getting lost. This idea is elegant,
especially when you start to think of the serifs as parasites living on the
letters, adding redundancy to the system in the form of blur (visual noise),
thus facilitating the communication. Some dyslexics are helped by
reading through a coloured filter, or by the use of a reading ruler. The
coloured filter appears to work by occluding certain wavelengths of light,
which cause the reader’s eye to dance or wander around the page. In other
words, by adding “noise” in the form of a filter that actually blocks some
of the visual spectrum, the message is able to get through more easily27.
One of the conventions of typography is that sans serif types have to be
tracked more tightly, with the words spaced more widely, so that a similar
effect of blurring is achieved: a convention perhaps deliberately flouted
by Tschichold to the ultimate disadvantage of the book and his thesis.
Similarly, the more vertical stress of the Modern types of Bodoni and
Didot means that they blur less effectively. You get a sense of lines
running down the page as well as across. Bodoni works well for headlines
and display, but is harder to read when used to set whole pages of text in
smaller sizes. With sans serifs and Moderns, the typographer is more
reliant on the quality of the print environment. An old-style serifed letter
like Garamond or even a “transitional” like Baskerville is more forgiving
of poor typography, narrow margins, and so on. Many of the older types
were designed in an era when the paper quality was poor and the ink
would bleed into the page, creating thicker, less contrasty strokes. This
may be because of the redundancy offered by the more oblique stress and
the bracketed serifs28.

The noise added by serifs, however, represents more
than just a simple blur. The presence/absence of
serifs can stand for degrees of authorial presence,
and the continued use of classic type designs points
towards ideas of authority and authenticity related
to that authorial presence which are absent from
more modern designs.

When the scribes at Tours began to use the Carolingian minuscules in
their revision of church books, they were reviving a letterform which
dated back at least 500 years. Given that their project involved returning
to original texts in order to create authentic, correct, uncorrupted copies,
it is no great leap to suppose that the Caroline minuscules stood, even
then, more more than simple legibility. They were also indications of
accuracy, pedigree and authenticity. Caroline minuscules are, by now,
letterforms that have been with us for 1700 years. They have a deepseated and comforting familiarity and a long-term association with
learning, civilisation, and culture. That this older and simpler letterform
was again returned to by the scholars of the Italian Renaissance in their
own revival of learning indicates two things. On the one hand, it seems
that a return to earlier letterforms runs parallel with scholastic exercises.
On the other hand, it seems that the clear and simple letterforms of the
roman letters based on Caroline minuscules (which in turn were based on
the earlier half-uncials) are particularly evocative of ideas surrounding
pains-taking, learning, research, and getting back to basics. Of course, it’s
also part of our civilisation that in every generation there are rule-breakers
and innovators who rail against the clichéd use of over-familiar
letterforms. They’re not wrong, but nor are they right. Their search for a
better letter misses the point that there can never be any such thing.
The longevity of the Carolingian letters represents an eddy in the flow of
linear time: part of the deep past that is still with us every day. The use of
an old-style type like Jenson’s roman is valid whenever a layout designer
wants to evoke elegance, or the human origin of words. For Jenson (or
Centaur) isn’t just ‘1470’ or even ‘1913’, but both, and neither: Janusfaced. Its use doesn’t take us back in time and leave us there – it simply
reminds us that we are not as ‘modern’ as we might think. Even the antihumanist impulse to abandon the Caroline and innovate is ancient and
very human.

A rejection of these classic forms, however, can represent not a flat
rejection of scholarship and accuracy, but a problematic embrace of
innovation, flair, modernity, and like the country rat, a preference for the
linear over the turbulent. This embrace is problematic because in spite of
the rhetoric of Jan Tschichold and the New Wave typographers, ideas of
‘newness’ and innovation are as old as typography itself. Aldus’ italic was
a new way of presenting printed information, and could justly be called
‘innovative’ in comparison to the contemporary gothic hand. Further,
Baskerville’s use of calendering and a truly black ink was as innovative as
the Parisian Romaine du Roi. Tschichold’s championing of collective
endeavour in type design was – again – nothing new when one considers
the committee that designed the Romain du Roi, or that type designs in
general have always been brought about collectively - Garamond working
with Granjon, and so on. Printing was always based upon imitation as its
first principle, with books meant to imitate handwritten books, type
designs being based upon other letterforms as a basic foundation, and a
whole chain of effort involved.

This chain of effort has always existed, from Jenson and Aldus through to
more recent designers like Robert Slimbach, who work alongside

specialists in computer code for digital type29. Type has always been the
space where the artist and the engineer meet, even if some have a
preference for one or the other.
Similarly, the popularity of ‘distressed’ typewriter-style faces in the 1990s
can be seen as an embrace of an older technology and an older style of
document, one that – ironically – looks more touched by human hands
than a modern, high-resolution digital revival of a ‘humanist’ typeface.
Here again, printing is based on imitation, in this case the kind of
document produced with a manual typewriter. In the 2010s, the fashion in
display type seems to be for similarly human-touched letterforms, based
on traditional commercial sign-writing or poster design.

irresponsible banking, a changing climate, a fragmented society and a
world in which every innocuous commuter journey could end in disaster.

In considering the matter of trends in type, readability, and related
matters, we are concerned both with questions of time and interference.
Serres points out that each of his books ‘describes a relationship, often
expressed by a unique preposition.’ Inter-ference is a relationship
expressed by ‘the spaces and times that are between32.’ In other words, he
is interested in media: the intermediate technologies and materials
through which messages have to pass.

Parasites are noise, noise is interference,
interference is mediation.
Using a very broad brush, a sliding scale can be proposed, to enable us to
‘read’ a typeface, and understand the reasons for its use in a particular
printing environment. In general terms, an older typeface usually has
more humanistic elements in it, and so can be said to suffer from greater
interference; more recent (post-industrial revolution) designs carry less
humanistic/parasitical baggage. As I mentioned in Part One, when used in
certain ways, type can assert its presence, and compensate for the Platonic
fear of ‘dead repetition, a repetition of the word cut off from its source’
(see introduction). For display purposes in particular, the presence of the
interference is more important than any other concern. In a display
environment, it is often desirable to increase the noise to a point where
attention is drawn to the carrier rather than – or as well as – the message.
This is particularly crucial when a brand identity is being established,
when the ‘voice’ ‘speaking’ the message matters more than what it says.
Some companies, as we have seen, go down the root of having logos, or
even a corporate typeface: like the one Apple Computers used to have:

The display environment has, of course, been of paramount importance to
the 20th and 21st centuries. Most contemporary typeface design is
concerned with novelty and display, but brand-new book faces do appear
occasionally33. Aside from the requirements of advertising, the
presentation of information can be achieved with greater or lesser levels
of interference. When an authorial presence is required, adding
interference in the form serifs on letters, or other design quirks, are de
rigeur. When the clear presentation of information in a fairly informal or
friendly voice is required, a humanistic sans serif is ideal. When the
message itself is the most important thing, and other concerns such as
authenticity, authority, and friendliness would perhaps get in the way, then
one of the industrial sans serifs will do the job.

Readers do ‘read’ typefaces, we are all trained to do it: we know that the
different alphabets have their special purposes, as they always have. We
know that SOMETHING PRINTED IN ALL CAPITALS is either
important, or was considered so by whoever laid it out. A capital letter
starts a sentence, or denotes a proper name. We know this, and understand
it without even thinking about it, but these special uses of the Roman
inscriptional majuscule are only conventions. Similarly, we understand
that something printed in italics is somehow supplementary to the
information around it in roman letters. In printing, italics can denote the
title of a book or other work, or the special use of a word, or can denote
emphasis as opposed to SHOUTING. Typographers will often use small
caps34 to denote page titles, or to lay out pages of contents, or to indicate
a raised voice in passages of dialogue:
“WHAT ON EARTH ARE YOU DOING‽”

Following on from these uses, all of which require the reader to know and
understand the conventions, it is but a small step to understand that the
use of a particular form of the roman letter – the humanist, or the modern
roman; the industrial or humanist sans serif – is also something that we
can read and draw meaning from. Rather than the always-doomed attempt
to abolish convention, to tear down the house and rebuild it without rats,
the focus for innovators should be on improving the reading experience in
special situations. In 2013, the greatest challenge facing typographers is in
formatting text for electronic books. With so many competing platforms
and technologies, a universal solution is extremely difficult. In 1996,
Bruno Maag was suggesting word-pictures, but it seems to me that a way
for book designers to embed a typeface (or at least those glyphs that were
actually used) so that an electronic book will display as intended would be
a far more elegant solution. The barrier to this is more about licensing and
fears of piracy than it is technological. Type foundries might want to be
compensated each and every time a type is downloaded, but my own

feeling is that the market would open up considerably if you could embed
fonts in an ebook. Vendors should forget about schemes to charge the end
user and focus on creating a culture in which beautiful books are valued –
even if they’re in electronic form.35
As for type reflecting the zeitgeist, this argument needs to be turned on its
head to be understood. It is not so much that type designed in 2013 must
be used for work printed in that year, but that certain type design choices
speak of values, attitudes and contexts which are specific to a time. In the
example given above of the fonts Populaire and Number Five, although
they’re inspired by different eras and reflect different attitudes and
responses, they both arise out of the same context, which can be traced
back to the economic shock of 2008. Setting the same words in different
typefaces can communicate a great deal:

dancers; the other a tuxedoed swing orchestra, a shiny dance floor and
champagne. Why either of these choices might be made, however,
depends on the economic, social, and historical context in which the type
is used. Understanding what these types evoke works only by convention,
though perhaps it is a convention based upon a knowledge of when a

improved, and yet this style of type will forever evoke ‘the future’ of our
dreams: the future of jet packs and flying cars that never becomes reality.
Ultimately, its use becomes hackneyed. The choice of type for the title
sequence of Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 film 2001: A Space Odyssey ends up
being far more interesting:

The font used, in case you don’t recognise it, is Gill Sans (Light), with the
non-circular zeros in the date replaced with capital Os. A humanist type,
then, but used in all caps, which gives it the appearance of a monoline
“industrial” font. It’s the same font used for “Keep Calm and Carry On”
but in a lighter weight. It has a quiet authority and clarity, but also a
certain neutral indifference. Some of the posters used to advertise the film
however clearly had less direct supervision from the famously controlling
director: they use Futura, which (I’m sure) would have been too obvious a
choice for the film’s title sequence. Gill Sans is much more interesting.
You might argue that in the 21st century, such ideas have fallen apart. So
much choice is available to so many people that anything goes. When I
originally wrote this in 1996, the “anything goes” idea was supported by
the huge variety of lettering in use at the time. In 2013, it’s fair to say that
Comic Sans has a certain dominance: used unthinkingly by people who
just want to use something “friendly”. On the other hand, the sales and
marketing operations behind type are much more sophisticated. There are
more online vendors of type than there were in 1996, and they
successfully feed the insatiable commercial desire for novelty with
beautifully rendered email shots richly illustrated with type samples.
Back in 1996, however, I noticed that the most common font in use was
Gill Sans, seen everywhere from local authority swimming pool leaflets to
building society signage, and – especially – job advertisements. In 2013,

Gill Sans is still everywhere: on those popular posters, for a start, and it
does seem to keep cropping up, especially during economically straitened
times.
Humanist types do indeed seem to crop up at times of economic
uncertainty. Gill sans was struck as type in the depression era of the
1930s; Hermann Zapf’s Optima comes from the postwar utility of Europe
in the 1950s. And there was Gill Sans again in 1996, being used to
advertise vacancies for the unemployed, and once more post-2008, telling
us all to keep calm while the kakistocracy plunders the earth for its
resources.
Humanist sans serif letters might stand as islands of clarity amid the
fragmented chaos of our age: so much hindsight tells us, anyway. It’s even
with hindsight that we recognise the naïve 1960s optimism about the
future in the ‘futuristic’ computer type shown above. It turns out that in
the real future, as we live it now, space belongs to the communication
satellites of earthly corporations; furthermore, space is full of junk.
Following the conventions provided by hindsight, and providing that the
layout designer and the reader understand the code (and even if we do not
know why, we can recognise the ideas evoked by certain letterforms, as in
the examples above), letters can take us backwards and forwards in time
very effectively. Just as they represent areas of stable flux in the
information system, they represent temporary areas of stability in the
chaotic movement of time. That it is so easy to evoke time and place in
this way indicates that the past is not so distant, and not so lost, as ideas of
renaissance and enlightenment would have us believe.
For Serres, time is like this: not a train on a one-way track implying that
where we have been is past, and that we are about to arrive at Time’s
destination. This linear view of time is what leads people to feel the need
to revive things in a renaissance, because they aren’t what they used to
be. This same view of time leads others to believe that now is better than
before, that we can solve the problems our ancestors failed to crack,
because we are better, smarter, faster. Is linear mindset is what creates
ideas of epistemological rupture: those moments when you cry Eureka!,
and the world turns upon its head, with everything changed: everything
old to be dumped (and later revived), everything new to be praised.

When Gutenberg started his printing press, it wasn’t an epistemological
rupture. It didn’t create Gutenberg Man, a new being who had never
existed before. Gutenberg didn’t so much create a new technology as
amalgamate several old ones; and the first book to be printed was the
Bible, not a treatise explaining the needs and responsibilities of New Men.
Ever since Gutenberg, however, each new wave of technology – in
printing as elsewhere – has been hailed as the next Copernican
Revolution. The Apple Macintosh was The Computer That Changed
Everything36, but it incorporated ideas dating back to the second world
war. Now we are told to forget books, that the future is online, on screen,
that a new and revolutionary form of type is required to meet the
requirements of the new-age. But you can still buy a second hand
paperback the pennies, and read it in the bath, on the toilet, on the beach
and on the train, and not care too much if it drops down a great big hole in
the road, or in the bath – in much the same spirit as Aldus meant his cheap
italic editions to be read.
The presence of roman letters reminds us of Serres’ belief that le temps
(time) is chaotic like le temps (weather): reminds us that we are always as
ancient as we are modern, as reliant on our old beliefs as we are our new.
And even the use of the most mathematically exact, industrial, sans-serif
letterform can remind us of this, because these forms are neither essential
nor exact, but belong to some fuzzy area in between. A sans-serif is just
an alternative to a serif: that fact is built into its very name. There can be
no such thing as an essential letterform, because the very existence of
rationality depends upon the anexactitude of the written symbol, takes
into account the human tendency to waver, to flourish – to fall over, like
the tower of Babel. Reason needs to take account of this falling over, this
stuttering, this rat in its foundations – just as mathematicians have been
grappling with chaos in recent years. The system works because it does
not work.
Anybody who uses type is occupied with a system in flux. The style and
disposition of the information on the printed page matters just as much as
the tone of a speaker’s voice. A page can be made to (figuratively)
mumble, or shout, whisper or scream. Reason always consigns emotions
to the realm of the irrational, when really our emotional response (or
absence thereof) is important to the success or failure of a given
communication.

Although writers like Gill, Tschichold, Morison and Updike claim to be
promoting a ‘rational’ view of typography, it is their passion for letters
which carries their arguments forward. An understanding of the ways in
which letters can produce an emotional response is basic to good
typography. In spite of the ‘postmodern’ tendency to see everything in
degraded terms, where lost crafts are forgotten and ‘anything goes’,
typographers still work hard at making designs to evoke moods, feelings,
and ideas. When people like Bruno Maag express their hatred of
Helvetica, it’s because the over-use of these types means that just one
mood, one feeling, one idea, is being used over an over again.
Because we can do these things, we should: we can no longer pretend it
has nothing to do with us; your software is not a typewriter. We have the
tools, and the reason for any degradation in quality lies within us. The
black box has been opened, the forbidden fruit is in our hands, and good
communication depends on the engaged use of its secret weapon.

Glossary of Terms
Baseline This term refers to the imaginary line upon which the letters
without descenders sit. Note that some letters, in order to look
optically even, actually dip slightly below this imaginary line,
even without descenders
Beard When type is struck, there always exists some space on the
punch or matrix around the letter design. The space beneath a
letter that does not register in print but which physically prevents
anything else from appearing there is known as the beard. With
digital type, of course, the physicality of type is no longer a
factor.
Bitmap The pattern of dots which ‘draws’ a letter on a computer screen.
PostScript fonts came with a bitmap ‘suitcase’ file, and a
separate ‘outline’ or printer file. Without this latter, printed
matter would render as jagged dots on a page rather than the
smooth curve of the original typeface design. A piece of software
from Adobe called Type Manager was developed to combine the
bitmap with the outline in order to create smooth screen displays.
TrueType and OpenType fonts combine the bitmap and outline in
one file.
Bleed
Pages are usually cut in order to fit the format of individual
publications, but they are printed before cutting so that when
printing is required right to the edge of a page, with no margin,
colour ‘bleeds’ beyond the cutting line to ensure evenness of
quality. This is because, usually, the edges of the paper are
printed only patchily by the machine, or are used for special
purposes such as colour matching.
Colour Refers to a particular shade of grey achieved on a page of type.
Viewed through slightly unfocused eyes, the colour of a page
depends upon such things as the size and weight of a type (from
light to heavy), the amount of leading used, the tracking, and so
on. Ideally, the colour of a page should be even, with the printed
area clearly distinguishable from the surrounding white space.
‘Rivers’ of white space down through the colour of the page are
indications of poor typography. They tend to be evident in

justified text – so you may see such rivers if you are reading this
in Amazon’s Kindle app, which (at the time of writing) gives the
reader no option to turn off justification.
Counters
The white space contained within the curves of closed
letters such as o and a but also open letters such as n and m.
Composition size types were those capable of being set by machine: up
to 14 point size.
Display sizes were over 14 points and had to be set by hand.
Extenders
The tails of letters like g, p, q, and y are known as
descenders, whereas the legs of letters like b and d are known as
ascenders. Legs and tails extend beyond the x-height. The
differences between types of the same point size often depend on
such factors as x-height, and the length of descenders and
ascenders. The longer the extenders, the more elegant a type
tends to look, whereas ‘legibility’ types have large x-heights and
short extenders. Some designs have ascenders higher than the
height of the capital letters, whereas others might match the
capital in height, or be shorter. With negative leading, extenders
tend to touch each other. You can see the differences between
types with differing x-heights by simply swapping – if possible –
between Palatino and Baskerville on your device.
Font
Deriving from the word fount, from foundry, this word is now
used interchangeably with typeface. Historically, however, a
single font is just one weight and size and style of a typeface.
Most modern typefaces now come in ‘families’ which will
incorporate several fonts of the same letter: light, book, medium,
bold, bold italic, extra bold, black, extra wide, narrow or
condensed, etc.
Glyph In each font, there are a number of individual letterforms,
known as glyphs. There are 26 lower-case letters in the roman
alphabet, for example, but there are a number of additional
glyphs for letters with accents (é è ç, and so on) and other
variations. Most standard QUERTY keyboards allow a skilled
and knowledgable typist to access 192 glyphs with various
keyboard combinations. These glyphs have always been easier to

access on a Mac keyboard. Software able to work with
OpenType fonts can make automatic substitutions on the fly.
Graphical User Interface (GUI) — this is sometimes known as the
‘desktop metaphor’, where instead of a blank screen upon which
they type commands in computer code in order to open, close,
and store files, the user is presented with icons representing
documents, applications and folders, which can be dragged
around the virtual space of the screen (the desktop) using the
‘pointer’ which is controlled by the ‘mouse’. With the GUI, the
simple action of dragging a document icon from one folder to
another using the mouse is the equivalent of typing coding
command to instruct the computer which directory on which
hard drive should be used to store a piece of work. With the GUI,
What You See Is What You Get (WYSIWYG) because of
bitmapping. (It’s quaint that in 1996 I felt the need to explain all
this – the desktop metaphor has now triumphed, and is even
considered outdated. On modern tablets and smartphones, users
get very little choice as to where documents are stored — the app
takes care of all that. This change is partly as a result of the
average user’s generally poor file management – files and
folders left everywhere, clogging the desktop, too many different
versions etc.)
Jaggies An effect of bitmapping, where the resolution of the computer
screen (which at one time was typically 72 dots per inch) meant
that on-screen type lost the smoothness of its outlines. Some
displays are now described in marketing literature as ‘retina’
resolution, which means that type displays as smoothly as it
would on paper. However, on a standard screen, the jagged effect
is controlled by anti-aliasing (where pixels of intermediate
colour are inserted, creating a slight softening effect). Before
laser and inkjet printers became ubiquitous, the jaggies could be
seen on the page when a dot-matrix printer such as Apple’s
ImageWriter was used. If you’ve ever seen a printout where the
font displayed in this rough and uneven way, it might have been
because the font’s outline file was missing.

Kerning In normal handwriting, letters such as f, y, and Q usually
overlap the letters next to them on a line, but with printing, the
necessary separation between individual letters in any font would
lead to unsightly gaps in the print. To get over this problem, type
designers created ‘kerned’ letters, with the horizontally
extending parts of the letters (kerns) designed to overlap. Some
technologies prevented the use of kerns, so some type designs
reveal truncated letters.

Samples of Photina, Times New Roman, Bembo and Eurostile, showing how the letters f and y have
more or less areas of overlap. With the pairings of We and To, the unsightly gap is what happens
without the built-in kerning that allows the overlap

In digital type, kerning now refers to the method whereby thousands
of ‘pairs’ of letters are created as part of the typeface, ensuring
that problematic pairs line up optically in tricky pairings like We
and To.
Leading The term used to indicate the amount of space between lines. In
the days of hot metal, in order to insert white space between
lines, the compositor would insert a blank line of lead which did
not register on the paper when in the press. Type set ‘solid’ is set
with no leading at all, while a typical situation is to see 10-point
type on 12 points of lead, usually expressed as 10/12 point. Too
much leading (for example, 10/18) will create a situation where
the reader’s eye finds it hard to move smoothly from the end of
one line to the beginning of the next. Too little will see the type
crowded too closely together, giving too dark a colour to the
page. The amount of leading it is best to use for legibility
depends upon the length of the extenders in a typeface, and its xheight. An irony of typography is that space saved in one
direction has to be lost in another in order to ensure legibility.
For example, a face with a large x-height and short extenders has
to be set with some leading, whereas type with a small x-height
and longer extenders can often be set solid, or with only one

point of lead, with no loss of legibility. Again, a condensed face
requires some leading to avoid making the colour of the page too
dense. Most people are not aware of it, but most word processing
programmes on personal computers offer the option of changing
the line spacing from th traditional typewriter carriage-return
units of 1, 1½ , and 2 lines, to line spacing calculated in points.
Changing over to this system allows one to be much more
precise, though designers have little control over the appearance
of text on some eBook reading devices.
Ligature Some letters are hard to pair up, even with kerning, so the
typeface will include pre-made pairs, such as fi and fl, or archaic
forms like æ and Æ

Linotype and Monotype were competing technologies, both aimed at
automating the task of typesetting. The Linotype machine
created slugs of type in lines after a keyboard operator had
punched in the letters in the correct combination. The Monotype
machine was much more complex, and produced a printed paper
punch which was then run through a second machine which
arranged type in individual letters. Linotype was favoured by
newspapers but had a limited selection of special sorts (symbols).
The Monotype machine produced high-quality output and was
favoured by the book trade. The Mergenthaler Linotype machine
was first demonstrated in 1886 in the office of the New York
Tribune. The Lanston Monotype machine was invented in 1887.
Old Style
One of the many different ways that type is categorised. It
belongs to the Lawson system, which categorises type faces as
follows: Old Style, Transitional, Modern, Slab Serif, Sans Serif,

Script, Display. Other classifications distinguish between
Venetian old styles and later ‘Garamond’ types etc.
Opening The appearance of two pages of a book, the left and the right,
viewed as a complimentary pair. Facing pages usually mirror
each other. Your word processor probably allows you to make
‘facing pages different’. Again, with e-readers, this is no longer a
design concern.
OpenType
This is the most recent of many different file formats that
have been used for digital type. The ultimate aim is to create a
system for setting electronic type that is as flexible and
customisable as setting type by hand. In 1470, Jenson had a
number of different glyphs available for each letter, meaning he
could adjust letter pairings by hand to make each page look both
natural and beautiful (he was, after all, trying to create a
facsimile of a handwritten book). OpenType allows a typeface to
contain more than 65,000 glyphs, meaning that software written
to take advantage of this can make automatic substitutions, either
creating a natural variation in letterforms or including fractions,
ligatures, and other alternates.

Left: standard letters, with no OpenType features; right: automatic substitutions with contextual
alternate characters

(Xerox) PARC—Palo Alto Research Center in California is a legend in
the history of computing. Established in 1970 and funded by
Rank Xerox, PARC’s remit was to invent the office of the future.
Xerox assembled there the best computing minds who proceeded
to develop the GUI: the mouse, windows, bitmapping etc., all
using a high-resolution display. Famously, Xerox failed to
capitalise on their own investment in these developments, though
they did produce an $18,000 computer called the Star which

incorporated many of them. It was left to Apple and then
Microsoft to popularise the ideas, and Apple at least did so using
many of the staff from PARC.
Point Sizes Since the 19th century, this has been the standard for
expressing the size of types — although there were differences
between the European and American point systems, due to the
difference in inches between continents. There are 12 points to a
pica, 72 to an inch. The standardisation of type around point
sizes replaced the quaint national systems which contained such
terms as Pica, Cicéro, Mediaan, Lettura (all expressing what we
now know as 12-point), and Great Primer, Long Primer, Small
Pica, Double Pica, Petit Canon, Parangon, Philosophie,
Nonpareille, etc.
Resolution Typically calculated in dots per inch (dpi) for printers or,
more recently, pixels per inch (ppi) for computer displays.
Apple’s ImageWriter printer, which came out with the early
Macintoshes, printed bitmaps at only 72dpi (the same as the then
screen resolution), or 144 dpi in ‘best’ mode. A low-end inkjet
printer in the 1990s would produce work at 360dpi, whereas a
basic laser printer of the same vintage would have been capable
of 600dpi (with more expensive 1200dpi versions available).
Print bureaux offered imagesetters, which could print at
resolutions between 1000 and 3000dpi. Wide format inkjet
printers, which measure their speed in square feet per hour, are
typically working between 720 and 1440 dpi. Because of the
differences in technology, an inkjet at 1440 dpi (with the correct
paper) produces output without any visible dots. At 300dpi, a
printer puts down 90,000 dots per square inch; at 1440dpi, the
output is 2.07 million dots per square inch. This is the difference
between low quality and high quality output. Some typefaces
have designs so subtle and delicate that they demand the bestpossible print quality. Optima is one example.
Tracking In order to make words fits upon a justified line of type, they
can be squeezed more closely together or spread further apart.
Adjustments can be made both to the spaces between words, and
to the space between letters. Basic word processing programmes

generally allow one level of condensation and one of extension,
but a full desktop publishing package will allow much finer
adjustments, so that a reader would probably not be aware of any
change. Some type designs are fairly loose, and the look of a
page can be improved considerably by tightening the letter
spacing. This is particularly useful for sans serif designs, which
if too widely spaced can look like unrelated individual letters.
Another solution to the problem is to use a special font which has
a lighter weight and/or a narrower shape. Conversely, in order to
give a headline or title more impact, a heavier weight and an
extended shape can be used. While DTP programmes will allow
adjustments in weight and width to be made, a high-quality effect
is achieved using a font which has been especially designed for
the purpose. These tracking adjustments can be made to the
spaces between words (word spacing) or to the individual letters.
Most traditional typographers would only ever use letter spacing
on the capital letters.
x-height Some letters, such as a and o often dip slightly below the
baseline in order to accommodate their curves whilst maintaining
the appearance of even height. The most reliable method of
determining the basic height of a font, disregarding its extenders,
is to measure the height of a lower-case x, which sits flat on the
baseline and has no extenders.

16. 15 For 400 years after Gutenberg invented moveable type, printers
composed lines of type by hand, one letter at a time.
When the printing was completed, the letters were returned to a type case.
And after 400 years of waiting for an efficient automated form of
typesetting, two rival technologies emerged at once. The Linotype machine
appeared around 1884 and the Monotype system arrived in 1887. There’s a
useful documentary about the Linotype
(https://itunes.apple.com/gb/movie/linotype-the-film/id566509420). The
main difference between the two systems is implied by their naming. The
Linotype machine set type in whole lines (or slugs); the Monotype machine
set individual letters. The faster Linotype machine was used in newspaper
and magazine printing, whereas the higher-quality Monotype system was
used in setting type for books. Monotype, in reviving types, did not return
to a single source, and revived versions of Garamond, Bell, Baskerville,
