A History of Data-Flow Languages
PAUL G. WHITING
ROBERT S.V. PASCOE
Data-flow refers both to a language-level paradigm of computation and to
a family of processor architectures based on this paradigm. This article
elaborates data-flow language issues and the evolution of data-flow languages. In considering limits to the expressive power of these languages,
underlying architectural issues are of necessity discussed. Although the article attempts to present a complete history of data-flow languages, it concentrates on those languages that specifically belong to this class and have
been implemented for a data-flow machine. In many cases, the distinctions
between issues of language semantics and machine architecture are unclear. Usually we have found that this reflects the evolution of data-flow, and
the close association between language and architecture development. In
some sections of the article, it may appear that there is an imbalance in the
amount of detail presented when compared with other sections. This imbalance is proportional to the publications and the amount of information readily
available for the topics.

D

ata-flow languages derive from a base paradigm of the
datu-flow graph - that is, the modeling of a program
as a set of operator nodes interconnected by a set of datacarrying arcs. Under this paradigm there is no current operation, and each operator is free to execute when its data
arrives. The interpretation of the language is naturally concurrent, and the concurrency is inherently fine-grained.
The data-flow model has the single-assignment property.
Values are data tokens that are transported from their producing node to the node that consumes them; there is no
concept of a variable with a state that can be arbitrarily updated at a later time. In data-flow, identifiers may be used to
name these data tokens. Such identifiers are thus either undefined (not yet produced) or carry a single unique value;
they cannot be updated.
For a counterpoint to data-flow, languages belonging to
the class referred to as functional languages model programs
as nested mathematical functions. In pure functional languages, identifiers denote subexpressions, and given that a
sub-expression has a single unique value (which cannot be
updated), these languages likewise share the singleassignment property.
In examining the development of data-flow languages, a
strong mutual influence between data-flow and functional
languages appears. This is probably due to their contemporary development, the sharing of certain fundamental properties that stood in stark contrast to the popular imperative
languages of their time, and similar problems of efficient
implementation shared by both language classes.
Most modern data-flow languages do not have their semantics defined in terms of program graphs. There are fundamental limitations of such a strict definition of data-flow.
In the first place, certain programming language facilities

that have proved to be useful (such as iteration) are not easily represented in terms of a static data-flow graph. Second,
the efficient implementation of certain operations, particularly those associated with creating and manipulating large
data structures, is fundamentally difficult in a pure data-flow
environment. We thus consider that data-flow languages are
those which, however their semantics are defined, have a
strongfiavor of the data-flow model, albeit as modified by
the evolutionary process. We also note that, although the
purity of the original model of data-flow has been sacrificed,
most language designers have sensibly attempted to preserve
equivalent semantics to the single-assignment property.
Finally, we have had several requests from reviewers to
include comments on data-flow hardware items, or the porting of non-data-flow languages to data-flow architectures.
We are concerned only with languages, and any mention we
make of hardware or architectural issues is only to support
the language comparison. Having said this, it is important to
note the close relationship between language and architecture in data-flow. The data-flow graph model of the language
initially suggested a model of (possibly simulated) networked processors as the implementation platform. Subsequent generations of the language have typically proceeded
with some new language feature being tested on an appropriate architecture, or with a new architectural facility being
tested against a language construct. The fine interplay between language and architecture is deeply ingrained in any
historical perspective of data-flow languages.

In comparison with the dynamic and hybrid models, several data-flow researchers were attracted to the static model
by its purity. In particular, the formal proof of program

more complicated as it introduces the concept of a color, or

waits for tokens with matching colors to arrive at its inputs

more potential concurrency. In particular, the color guarantees the appropriate matching of tokens in certain situations
in cyclic graphs, and, depending on the language implementation, is of importance in facilitating natural recursion and
loop execution. This is despite the fact that (according to
Dennis) efficient implementation of recursion on massively
parallel architectures remains an open issue. Colors may also
ensure that tokens partake only in operations with the correct partners - a possible problem for static architectures.
The dynamic model also removes the synchronization
(handshaking) between nodes that is necessary in the static
model to prevent nodes from producing tokens on an occu-

pied arc. This may not, however, yield much benefit; some
form of handshaking may be necessary in the queued models
(both static and dynamic) to prevent queue overflow.

Data-flow computers
In a 1980 paper,'* Dennis described data-flow computers
as radically different from the majority of parallel computers:
0

.
.
0

An instruction is ready for execution when its operands
are available.
All instructions are functions (i.e., there are no side effects).
There is no concept of control flow, and as a result
there is no need for a program counter.
Information is transmitted rather than stored in memory.
As there are no global shared resources, there are no
bottlenecks caused by resource allocation. (Data-flow
computers are not, however, without their own very
significant resource allocation problems.)

It should be noted that pragmatic issues (typically associated with efficiency of implementation of large data structures) have led many actual data-flow implementers to violate Dennis' rules. They strive, however, to preserve
equivalent semantics in their implementations. With these
points in mind, a data-flow computer can be said to consist
of three major components: instruction memory that contains
all instructions in the data-flow graph, a set of processing
elements that perform the operations specified by each instruction, and a network that carries both the instruction
packets and the result packets to the appropriate processing
elements.

Programming data-flow machines
Many of the early publications on data-flow execution
models represented programs simply by drawing graphs,
referred to as data-flow nets, networks, graphs, or schemas.
To actually program a data-flow machine, there were several
options available to researchers: use of an existing language
without concurrency control, extensions to an existing language by the addition of concurrency control, or the design
of a new language.
The first option has been discussed by many researchers
as not being suitable for a number of reasons. However,
according to Dennis, the group at Texas Instruments adopted
a subset of the Fortran language because of Fortran software
and expertise available from earlier compiler implementation work.
The efficient extraction of parallelism from programs
written in imperative languages was probably beyond the
software technology available at the time, and, although
imperative languages have been implemented on data-flow
machines, they were rather too distinct from the data-flow
paradigm to have been popular. Indeed, even in 1994, the
efficient implementation of imperative languages on dataflow machines remains an open research topic.
The second option was based on languages with explicit
constructs for concurrency control, such as Per Brinch Hansen’s Concurrent Pascal, Niklaus Wirth’s Modula, Ada, and
the implementation of Hoare’s CSP through Occam.46
Though this technique of extending languages has been successful for providing programming languages for other platforms, data-flow researchers have felt that this seems
“unnatural” to programmers. The particular deficit of this
approach is that it forces programmers to explicitly partition
and control concurrency, thus negating a principal benefit of
the data-flow model. In the data-flow model, concurrency is
implicit and its control transparent to the programmer.
Therefore, it is less prone to errors of deadlock and unintentional indeterminate results.
The third option has proved to be the most popular with
advocates of data-flow. In some cases, the language has been

At various stages in the conduct of data-flow research,
studies have been carried out using languages from the imperative, functional, logic, and graphical classes of programming languages. However, the oldest and most successful languages for programming data-flow machines have not
come from any of these. Instead, researchers have adopted
functional languages and added a flavor of imperative languages (particularly for loop handling) to produce the new
class of data-flow languages.

What constitutes a data-flow language? The features of
a data-flow language are listed in Ackerman’s 1982 papep’
as:
1. freedom from side effects,
2. locality of effect,
3. equivalence of instruction scheduling with data dependencies,
4. single-assignment semantics,
5. an unusual notation for iterations because of features 1
and 4, and
6. a lack of history sensitivity in procedures.
For comparison with conventional languages, a data-flow
language generally has the following properties:
declarations bind names to values on a once-only basis;
there is no flow of control on a statement-by-statement
basis - statements are not executed in textual order,
but rather according to data dependencies;
the principal unit of modularization is the function,
which, in common with purely functional languages, is
textually equivalent to the value it denotes;
concurrency is implicit and therefore not highlighted in
the language; and
while the specific details of V O vary from language to
language, its provision is difficult since the lack of
deterministic execution order either prevents sequential
YO or requires an explicit sequencing construct in the
language.
Some form of artificial sequencing is generally introduced,
if indeed the language specifies VO at all. Many language
designers were quite content to treat input data as constants
in the program, and to inspect values produced by monitoring the underlying implementation architecture. Possibly the
most satisfying approach to sequencing VO, where the language includes a stream data construct, is to simply map the
ordered stream onto an output device.

Lucid treated assignment statements as equations and
made loops implicit by imposing restrictions on the use of
assignment. These restrictions meant that an identifier in a
program could have only one specification: direct or inductive. An identifier specified directly could be assigned only
once in a Lucid program. If an identifier was specified inductively, the restriction was that it could be assigned only
twice, once for initialization and once for updating. This also
meant that every loop identifier had to be updated, whether
the value was changed or not. This treatment of identifiers
within loops has become an important convention for the
data-flow community. In effect, each iteration has a distinct
identifier that receives its value from an expression involving the same-named identifier in the previous iteration (e.g.,
the next assignment construct in the example). This has two
important consequences for data-flow languages: Loop identifiers now obey the single-assignment rule; and iteration is
equivalent to tail recursion, with the next values being
transmitted as parameters - this also allows loop unfolding
so that loops can execute in parallel, limited only by the data
dependence from the previous level of loop identifiers.

FP. In 1978, John Backus published a very important paper for research in both functional languages and data-flow
computing.68 Backus explained that conventional languages
had grown too large and awkward, and were creating unnecessary confusion in the way programmers thought about
programs. They were designed around the model of the von
Neumann computer and turned programming into the task of
planning and detailing the traffic of data through the von
Neumann bottleneck. The dependence on conventional languages had made the development and use of non-von Neumann architectures uneconomical and had deprived computer architects of an intellectual foundation for new
computers. Backus then went on to introduce three possible
replacements for conventional languages:
1. functional programming (FP systems),
2. formal systems for functional programming (FFP systems), and
3. applicative state-transition systems (AST systems).
The first and simplest of these systems did not use identifiers and consisted of objects, functions, applications
(operations), functional forms (for combining functions),
and definitions (to define and name functions). The main
problems associated with FF' systems surfaced because they
were not history sensitive (which could be overcome by the
other two systems), and they had limited use in solving
problems due to the set of primitive functions and functional
forms they contained. This was felt to be an advantage, however, as it allowed FP systems to overcome the problems
associated with naming systems in conventional languages
and the substitution rule of lambda calculus.
The main difference between FP and FFP systems was the
ability of the second to define new functional forms. This
provided them with a great deal of flexibility over the FP
systems, particularly as the power of an FP system was defined by the set of its functional forms.
An AST system consisted of three elements: an applicative subsystem (such as an FFP system), a state D
(containing the set of definitions of the applicative subsystem), and a set of transition rules describing how the inputs
were transformed into outputs and the changes to state D.
These combined to allow AST systems to have a number of
unique characteristics. The most important of these was that
states could not change during a computation. This prevented side effects and implied independent applications
could be evaluated in parallel. Other benefits included state
transitions occumng only once per major computation
(which eliminated the von Neumann bottleneck), increased
flexibility and power over conventional languages, and the
ability to define appropriate functions and introduce new and
improved methods for structured programming. All this was

built on a fixed framework, tiny in comparison with those
used by conventional languages.
Although Backus did not include any formal syntax of a
new language or any implementation details, his work argued strongly and convincingly for a change in attitude toward new succinct languages. It demonstrated the benefits of

Data-flow languages
Before discussing the individual data-flow languages, we
recall that the majority were influenced by functional languages. Single-assignment languages (SALS) such as Compels7 and Samples8 were also influential on data-flow languages.
The two classes share a number of common features, including the use of functions instead of procedures, recursion,
the order of statements not being important, and a side-

IEEE Annals of the History of Computing, Vol. 16, No. 4, 1994

effect-free environment. The importance of SALS lay in the
fact that a data item is assigned a value on only one occasion
(iteration is possible by the inclusion of a special feature to
distinguish between different instances of a loop identifier).
This makes it possible to detect every use of a value, because each statement is executed only once. It also makes
storage obsolete and enables independent computations to
proceed concurrently; obviously two appropriate features in
a language for use on data-flow machines.
The issue of allowing loops in the context of singleassignment semantics (and therefore data-flow) is often
solved by providing a new operator (or in some cases, the
use of the old operator in the binding expression), for example, new i = i + 1, which does not change i in the current
loop but defines i for the next cycle. The objects denoted by
i in each iteration are considered distinct.

TDFL. Kung-Song Weng (a.k.a. Ken), a master's student
at MIT, believed that conventional languages were not suitable for parallel processing, as they could express only limited amounts of parallelism and provided opportunities
where nondeterminacy and deadlock could arise. Therefore,
he set about designing a textual language with data-flow
schemas as its semantic basis and satisfying the following
requirements: a simple translation into data-flow schemas;
compile time check for deadlocks where possible; semantics
simple enough to be formalized; features for stream-oriented
computation; and features for expressing a system of interconnected modules (or processes) that communicate by exchanging data through communication channels.
The result was called the Textual Data-Flow Language
(TDFL).89 It was restricted to determinate problems, and its
only data types were integers and Booleans. No data structures were provided.
In TDFL, a program consisted of a list of module definitions followed by a list of statements separated by semicolons. A module, if defined recursively, used module as its
heading; otherwise, module was used. The interface to a
module outlined the formal parameters by explicitly defining
the input and output identifiers. Each interface was required
to have at least one input and output identifier. The type of
identifier was specified when the name was followed by a
type: either integer (int) or Boolean (bool). All identifiers in
a module interface had to be typed, while those inside the
module needed only to be typed when an ambiguity occurred. Such identifiers were considered to be well defined
in that they were defined before use (i.e., the language used
the concept of statement ordering not normally associated
with functional or data-flow languages).
TDFL statements were an assignment, a module call, or a
conditional statement (if" statement). An assignment complied with the single-assignment rule and was different from
the normal use in that the identifier being defined was on the
right. Iteration was not provided for two reasons: It did not
have a corresponding mathematical notation, and it involved
the update of an identifier and this did not conform to single
assignment. Figure 2 shows a simple recursive module in
TDFL.

scope of access for that identifier. Functions could not use
global values and were able to return more than one value.

declared. Recursion was not provided; this was probably due
to the expected domain of application (scientific programming).

DDN. Data Driven Nets (DDN) was the language designed
by A1 Davis to program his DDMl ~omputer.’~
A DDN program was represented internally as a well-nested, parenthesized, variable-field-length, character string. Both the machine and the language were the results of early work
described in Davis’ PhD thesis.26 DDNs were first described

collection of directed data paths interconnecting these cells.
Typed data items - including program, message, vector,

concurrency to be extracted from the program. Another feature of DDNs is that the distribution of the program graph

GPL. The Graphical data-driven Programming Language
(GPL) was designed by Davis and Lowder at the University
of Utah.72The main concept of GPL was based on the token
model, whereby the flow of data around a program graph
was viewed as a stream of tokens. The nodes in the graph
were referred to as functions, and there were two types:
atomic functions and macrofunctions. Atomic functions were
simple nodes, while macrofunctions were used for program
structuring and could be expanded to replace a single node
with the underlying atomic functions that made up the
macrofunction. Macrofunctions also enabled recursion. Iteration and conditional constructs were made available
through “selector” and “distributor” nodes. The data flowing
around the graphs could be typed by associating a type with
an arc. GPL also had capabilities for handling tuples, files,
and functions as values.
Davis explained (in a personal communication) that the
motivation behind GPL came from the fact that working in
low-level data-flow concepts (i.e., DDN programming) was
akin to assembler hacking. Having used DDN to understand
the fundamentals, he believed the time was right to focus his
efforts on a system that supported real problem solving. The
result was GPL, which Davis believes was the first fully
functional high-level graphical programming system to be
implemented. It provided program visualization and debugging. The design was influenced by LAU and permitted programmers to use graphs or text wherever they wanted, with
the text being written in a Lucid or LAU style (i.e., singleassignment).
FGL. Function Graph Language (FGL) was the system
created by Keller and Yen, also of the University of
Utah.73 FGL was different from GPL, in that instead of
working on the principle that values flowed around
graphs as a stream, values were grouped together in
structures and operated on as a single object. The benefit
of this approach was that it allowed a stream of values to
be dealt with as a whole rather than individually, and it
was said to be more flexible and easier to use as a result.
It had much the same operators as GPL, and included the
use of macrofunctions.
It was recognized that the model that GPL was based on
was more efficient than that used by FGL. The token-bytoken processing in the token model of GPL resulted in efficient memory management. On the other hand, the structure
model of FGL used fully general storage management,
which recycled storage in a more costly manner. It was
hoped that compiler optimization and special execution
techniques would improve FGL’s underlying efficiency
without penalizing its generality.

Comparison of language features
It is fruitful to compare the features of each language that have
particular significance in the evolution of data-flow. (We are
indebted to Jack Dennis for the criteria we use here.) However,
we are comparing languages that were evolving as vehicles of
research. Some of the languages were never implemented, and
none reached the type of standardization process typical of more
popular languages. Often, particular attributes of languages were
not defined in the published literature, and - in one or two cases
- could not be determined by contact with the authors. Some
language designers, such as Lucid’s authors, explicitly acknowledge that certain aspects of the language semantics are determined by the implementation. This is not to be taken as a comment on the authors’ acumen, but simply as recognition of the
fact that the language defmitions were products of dynamic research projects.

The data-flow graph model. The choice of model falls
particularly into the static or dynamic schemes, or a hybrid
of both. This choice is available when the underlying language semantics are based directly on a data-flow model.
Many languages, however, though strictly data-flow by our
earlier classification, derive their semantics from other than
a graph model. Since the emphasis here is on language issues rather than execution platforms, we do not classify
these languages in the sense of requiring a particular architecture for implementation.
The principal languages adhering to the static model are
LAU, VAL, and TDFL. The dynamic languages are LAPSE
and Valid. LAU has underlying semantics derived from the
single-assignment property. Id was originally based on
Dennis’ static model; from 1977 the semantics were expressed in terms of the U-interpreter. Arvind (in a personal
communication) emphasized that the later semantics of Id
were defined as graphs at the language level, rather than in
terms of an underlying graph model. Lucid - though influenced by the work of Wadge and Davis - was originally not
intended to run on data-flow machines. Indeed, aspects of
the operational semantics were clearly stated to be left to the
implementer. Although an implementation of Lucid was
developed for Manchester’s dynamic architecture,81Ed Ashcroft said (in a personal communication) that the underlying
model of Lucid is demand-driven evaluation rather than the
data-driven evaluation of data-flow.
SISAL is interesting, and one might initially expect it to
use a static graph model since it is a descendant of VAL.
SISAL is translated to an intermediate form (IFl), which is
not directly related to any data-flow graph model. This allows various implementations of the language, which are
really dependent on the features of the target architecture to
which the language is being ported. It is important to remember that the first implementation of SISAL was for the
Manchester data-flow machine, which used the dynamic
graph model.

static representation of the program (a graph) can have subgraphs that are directly or indirectly self-referential. While
the choice of the data-flow graph model can have implications for implementing recursion, the choice of the model
does not of itself allow or deny recursion.

ata-flow languages have been shown to be a combination of imperative and functional languages, often
having the look and feel of imperative languages with the
semantics of functional languages. As a sibling of the functional class of languages, they themselves contain a subset
- the graphical languages. They provide an elegant vehicle
in which to achieve implicit concurrency. With their avoidance of side effects and their use of the single-assignment
rule, they have satisfyingly clean semantics, and avoid many
of the errors associated with imperative languages. Achieving implicit parallelism is a most worthy objective of a language implementation, as it removes from the programmer
responsibility for tedious, error-prone work involving partitioning and the synchronization of subtasks. No class of
language achieves implicit parallelism better than the dataflow languages.
However, several of our respondents, for example, Davis,
indicated that data-flow has been “mostly.. .a failure.” Two
decades ago, the purity and quality of the language approach
were appreciated by the research community, and the question of really efficient execution was yet to be addressed. In
the interim, highly efficient, cost-effective data-flow architectures have failed to materialize - or at least failed to
achieve popular acceptance. In fact, the very fine grain parallelism of data-flow has proved, in the view of many researchers, to be ultimately a disadvantage. In addition, over
the last two decades, the scale of research and development
in data-flow has been almost totally overshadowed by the
momentum and investment in the microprocessor community. It is ironic that many of the successes of the latest microprocessor designs stem directly from data-flow work.
But data-flow (both the language paradigm and the architecture) has had real successes, even if it has not yielded
popular products. In particular, its influence on language
evolution, compilation technology for other languages, and
processor architecture design has been important. Examples
of this can be seen in the NEC image pipeline processors,”
multithreaded architectures, the way data-flow synchronization has worked its way into concurrent logic programming
languages, and some of the language features in the new
functional language H a ~ k e 1 l . l ~ ~
There are several research cells still pursuing the realization of data-flow. Whether data-flow will simply fade away
to leave no more than a legacy for other developments, or
whether the basic qualities of the data-flow (and functional)
languages will reassert themselves, cannot be known in this
decade. We wait with interest.
